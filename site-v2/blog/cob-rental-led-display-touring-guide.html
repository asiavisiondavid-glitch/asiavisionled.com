Touring breaks an LED screen in ways a fixed install never does. A rental panel is rigged, struck, road-cased, trucked, dropped back onto a stage and rebuilt again — often indoors on Friday and outdoors on Saturday. The enclosure that survives that cycle is the one that decides whether the tour stays on schedule.

This guide covers what actually changes when a screen goes on the road, why chip-on-board (COB) panels are increasingly specified for rental and touring work, and which cabinet and service questions decide whether a fleet still looks right after a season of use.

Why Rental Work Is Harder on a Display Than a Fixed Install

A fixed installation is judged on the day it is commissioned. A rental screen is judged every night, by a different crew, in a different venue. Between shows it spends hours in a road case and a truck, and it is handled far more often than any wall bolted to a lobby wall. The wear is not a single dramatic event; it is hundreds of small handling events, each one a chance to chip a surface, loosen a connector or trap moisture.

That is why rental buyers rarely compare pixel pitch alone. They compare how a panel behaves when it is lifted by its edges, stacked in a case, rained on at load-in and wiped down before doors open. The protective surface and the cabinet — not the brightness figure — decide how much of the fleet is still in service at the end of the year.

What COB Changes on the Road

In a conventional SMD module, individual LED lamps sit on the module surface with a wire bond and a solder joint exposed to the world. Those joints are the first thing to suffer when a panel is handled constantly. COB takes the opposite approach: the LED chips are bonded directly to the board and covered by a protective layer, so there is no exposed lamp bead or wire bond to knock loose. The full comparison of the three encapsulation methods is in our COB vs SMD vs GOB encapsulation guide.

On a tour this changes several practical things at once. The face can be wiped clean without the care a bare lamp surface needs. A knocked corner is less likely to take a lamp with it. Humidity at an outdoor load-in has a sealed surface to contend with rather than exposed joints. And because the surface is smoother and more uniform, a COB panel holds a cleaner look at close camera distance — which matters whenever the screen is filming as well as being watched.

The Cabinet Decides as Much as the Panel

Crews do not lift panels; they lift cabinets. Weight, the speed of the locking mechanism, how the cabinet stacks in a case and how it hangs from a truss all shape how fast a build goes up and how safely it comes down. A panel that is technically excellent but slow to assemble costs a tour real money in labour hours.

Rental cabinets are usually die-cast aluminium for a reason: it is light, it holds its shape after repeated handling, and it keeps tolerances tight enough that panels line up without shimming. Curved and straight sections sharing one locking system matter too, because most shows mix both. If you are comparing cabinet options in more detail, our rental cabinet selection guide walks through the trade-offs.

Touring specification checklist:
✅ Surface: sealed and cleanable, or exposed lamps?
✅ Handling: how many rig-and-strike cycles per year?
✅ Cabinet: weight per panel, and how fast a crew locks it?
✅ Rigging: truss load, hanging points and safety provisions?
✅ Environment: indoor only, or outdoor load-ins in the rain?
✅ Spares: how fast can a failed module be swapped mid-tour?

Failure Modes That Stop a Show

The failures that hurt on tour are rarely the dramatic ones. They are the small, repeated ones: a column that goes dark because a connector worked loose in transit, a panel that will not latch cleanly because a corner was deformed, a row that discolours because moisture found a way in. Each is minor on a bench and serious at load-in.

A sealed, chip-on-board surface removes several of those entry points at once, but it does not remove the need for a service plan. The panels most likely to fail are the ones that have been handled the most, so the practical question is not whether a panel can fail, but how quickly a crew can replace it without pulling the whole wall down.

Spares and Service on a Touring Schedule

A rental company buys a screen once and then lives with the spares decision for years. The useful question is how the fleet behaves when a single panel fails a week before a festival. If replacement modules ship from stock and the swap is a ten-minute job with the wall in place, a failure is an inconvenience. If the panel has to come out of the wall or wait for a batch, it is a scheduling problem.

That is why we treat spares as part of the specification rather than an afterthought. Buyers auditing a rental supplier should ask the same thing: what is the module swap procedure, how long does it take, and what is held in stock? Specifying a COB Screen for a Tour

Start from the show, not the spec sheet. A corporate roadshow, a festival stage and a broadcast set each place different demands on pitch, refresh and cabinet. Pitch follows the closest audience or camera distance; refresh follows whether the screen is filmed; the cabinet follows whether the show is indoors or out.

FAQ

Q: Is COB better than SMD for rental and touring?
A: For panels that are handled constantly, a COB face has fewer exposed parts to damage — the LED chips sit under a protective layer instead of on the surface with a wire bond and solder joint. That tends to mean a cleaner surface after a season of use and easier cleaning between shows. SMD remains the economical volume choice where handling is light and lamp-level service is preferred.
Q: What pixel pitch should a touring screen use?
A: Pitch follows the closest realistic viewing distance, not the longest. A corporate roadshow with front rows close in usually wants a finer pitch than a festival stage read from tens of metres. If the screen is also filmed, pitch and refresh both matter more than brightness.
Q: How important is cabinet weight on a tour?
A: Weight drives labour and rigging. Lighter cabinets mean fewer crew hours per build and less load on the truss, which matters most on shows with short turnaround. Die-cast aluminium cabinets are common in rental work because they are light, rigid and hold their shape after repeated handling.
Q: What spares should a rental company hold?
A: Enough modules to cover the failure rate of the fleet plus a margin, and a swap procedure that can be done with the wall in place. The key question is turnaround: a module that ships from stock and swaps in minutes is an inconvenience, a module that has to wait for a batch is a scheduling problem.
Q: Can a COB rental screen be used outdoors?
A: A sealed COB cabinet built for outdoor use can be, and that is what our touring range is for. The deciding factor is the cabinet's sealing and wind rating, not the encapsulation alone, so specify the cabinet for the environment the show actually plays in.
