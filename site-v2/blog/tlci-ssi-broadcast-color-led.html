What are TLCI and SSI for a studio LED wall? They are lighting-colour indices that a buyer can ask for when the wall is not only a picture surface, but also a light that falls on talent, costumes and set. The Television Lighting Consistency Index (TLCI) and the Spectral Similarity Index (SSI) describe how a source’s spectrum behaves for a camera or against a named reference illuminant. They are not the same as display gamut, white balance or contrast. The buying decision is whether the RFQ asks for those lighting data — and a camera test — or treats a DCI-P3 or “broadcast colour” claim as if it already answered the question.

This guide is for broadcast engineers, lighting directors, DPs and integrators specifying a camera-facing studio or volume wall. It covers only TLCI, SSI and the acceptance data that go with them. White balance and calibration belong in the white-balance calibration guide; batch-to-batch uniformity belongs in the colour-uniformity guide; Kelvin and white-point choice belong in the colour-temperature guide; contrast belongs in the contrast-ratio guide. For the studio wall as a system, use the broadcast studio buying guide. A completed studio is described in the Yle News Finland case. Transfer functions (HLG and PQ) are a different clause and are covered in the HDR, HLG and PQ specification.

Why Lighting Indices Are Asked for on a Wall

In a studio, the LED wall is often close enough to light the talent. Skin, wardrobe and props then take their colour from the wall’s spectrum as well as from the pictures on it. A wall can have a wide display gamut and still be a poor luminaire: a spiky or gappy spectrum can make a camera’s colour matrix work harder, or it can fail to match the house softlights and practicals. TLCI and SSI exist to describe that lighting behaviour. They do not replace a camera test, and they do not replace a display calibration report.

TLCI, published by the EBU as a method for lighting, scores how a source is expected to render a set of colours through a typical broadcast-camera model, against a reference. SSI, published by the Academy as a spectral similarity index, scores how closely a source’s spectral power distribution matches a named reference (for example a daylight or a black-body locus). Both are usually reported on a 0–100 scale. This article does not invent a pass mark, and it does not treat a catalogue “high TLCI” claim as a substitute for a named method, a named reference and a report on the proposed series.

What TLCI and SSI Are Not

They are not display-gamut figures. A published DCI-P3 coverage describes how much of that display colour space the wall can reach; it says nothing, by itself, about the spectrum that falls on a face in front of the wall. They are not a white-balance setting, a CCT, a contrast ratio, or an HDR transfer function. Those belong in their own clauses, written against the guides linked above. They are also not a promise that every camera will see the same result: TLCI uses a defined camera model, and a house camera with a different spectral sensitivity still has to be tested on the installed wall.

If the project only cares about the wall as a picture — a backdrop that is not lighting talent — a lighting-index clause may be unnecessary. If people will stand in front of it, or if the wall is used as a soft source, the clause is worth writing. Say which job the wall is doing, so the bidder is not asked for lighting data on a signage wall, or excused from them on a studio cyc.

That is the starting point for the RFQ: those data have to be requested and confirmed on the quotation for the proposed series, pitch and drive condition, or the acceptance has to be defined as a camera test on the wall. Do not infer a TLCI or SSI figure from DCI-P3 coverage, from contrast, from brightness, or from an anti-glare or anti-moiré description.

What to Ask for in Acceptance

A useful colour-acceptance clause separates lighting data from display data, and it names the condition:

  • a spectral power distribution, or a TLCI and SSI report, for the proposed series and pitch, at a stated luminance and white point, with the method and the reference illuminant named;
  • whether the report is for a single module, a cabinet, or a representative wall, and whether it applies to the exact drive mode you are buying;
  • the display side in its own clauses: target white point, gamut, uniformity and calibration method, written against the existing guides rather than restated here;
  • an on-camera test on the proposed or installed wall, with the house camera, the intended lighting plot, and a stated wardrobe or skin-tone check — because an index is a prediction, not the shot;
  • how the wall will be dimmed or limited when it is used as a source, so a full-brightness catalogue condition is not the only number on the page.

Do not copy a single “broadcast colour” or “camera-ready” sentence from a brochure into a bid. Two walls can both be described that way and still light a face differently. If a bidder offers a typical or catalogue TLCI or SSI value, ask whether it applies to the exact series, pitch and luminance you are buying, and have it confirmed on the quotation. Asia Vision will not invent a TLCI or SSI score that the product page does not publish.

What to Put in the RFQ

A workable RFQ states whether the wall is a light as well as a display, and it asks for named data:

Suggested RFQ wording:
“Where the LED wall will light talent or set, the bidder shall supply, for the proposed series and pitch, a spectral power distribution or a TLCI and SSI report at a stated luminance and white point, with the method and the reference illuminant identified. A published display gamut (including DCI-P3 coverage), contrast ratio or ‘broadcast colour’ claim is not accepted in place of those lighting data. Product-page specifications that do not include TLCI or SSI shall be confirmed on the quotation. Acceptance may include an on-camera test with the house camera and lighting plot.”

Attach the camera list, whether people will work in front of the wall, the intended white point, and any house lighting-index criterion you already use. If your colourist or lighting director has already set a minimum index or a reference illuminant, put that in the annex rather than asking the bidder to invent a pass mark. Product pages publish a 3-year warranty and 18-day standard delivery; a spectral report, a calibration visit or a camera test can take longer, and that time should sit in the programme.

FAQ

Q: Do Asia Vision product pages publish a TLCI or SSI score?
A: No. The E-PRO, RH-PRO COB and COB CV-PRO pages do not publish a TLCI score, an SSI score or a spectral power distribution. Ask for a report at a stated luminance and white point, or define acceptance as a camera test, and have that confirmed on the quotation.
Q: Does a DCI-P3 figure replace TLCI or SSI?
A: No. DCI-P3 coverage is a display-gamut figure. TLCI and SSI describe the wall as a light source. Write them as separate clauses, and do not treat one as a substitute for the other.
Q: Is a catalogue lighting-index enough for acceptance?
A: Not by itself. Ask whether the figure applies to the exact series, pitch and luminance you are buying, name the method and the reference, and include an on-camera test with the house camera where talent will be lit by the wall.
Q: Does this article replace the white-balance or contrast guides?
A: No. This page covers only TLCI, SSI and lighting-index acceptance for a studio LED wall. White balance, uniformity, colour temperature, contrast and HDR transfer functions are specified in their own guides.
