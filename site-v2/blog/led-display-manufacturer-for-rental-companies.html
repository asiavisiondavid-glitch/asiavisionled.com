A rental company buys LED panels differently from anyone else. The screen is not installed once and admired; it is loaded out, rigged, struck and packed, week after week, often by different crews. What decides whether that fleet makes money is not the peak brightness on a specification sheet — it is how the panel survives the handling and how fast a failure can be fixed.

This page sets out what a rental operation should look for in a manufacturer, and how Asia Vision builds for that kind of use.

What Rental Companies Actually Need

Five things matter more than headline specifications, and they are not the ones usually listed first in a brochure.

RequirementWhy it decides the economics
Panel durabilityKnocks in load-in and load-out are the most common damage; a face that resists them cuts repair cost across a season.
One cabinet platformA shared cabinet means one rigging method, one spare-parts set and one training standard across the fleet.
Spares and lead timeA show does not wait for a slow replacement. The resupply route matters more than the defect rate.
Service responseA fault on site is the moment the manufacturer is judged. Support has to exist at that point.
Predictable supplyA fleet bought in stages has to be extendable later without a visual or structural mismatch.

A panel that is technically excellent but hard to resupply is, in practice, more expensive than a plainer one that can be replaced quickly.

Durability: What Survives a Season

The failure that takes a show down is rarely a sophisticated electronics fault. It is the panel that was dropped, kicked or struck in transit, and the face that cracked when it happened.

That is why the surface technology matters for rental more than for fixed work. A chip-on-board (COB) face is bonded and sealed as a continuous surface rather than built from individual lamps, so there is no lamp to knock off and the front can be wiped clean. Our COB Z series is rated for roughly 3.5× the knock resistance of an equivalent SMD module — a figure that matters precisely in the environment a rental fleet works in.

Where the fleet is well cased and handled by experienced crews, a conventional SMD panel remains a sound and more economical option. The decision follows the handling pattern, not the technology in isolation.

Fleet Standardisation

A rental business grows panel by panel. If every purchase introduces a different cabinet, the fleet accumulates rigging methods, spare-parts bins and crew knowledge that do not transfer. That overhead is invisible on a purchase order and very visible in daily operations.

The practical answer is to standardise on one cabinet family that offers the sizes the work actually needs. Our rental range is built on a 500×500mm and 500×1000mm platform, so a fleet can cover both small stages and large walls without running two systems. Where the product family shares locking, power and flight-case dimensions across models, a replacement panel bought a year later still fits the rig.

The question worth asking a supplier: if I buy panels from you in twelve months, will they lock into the frames I buy today? A manufacturer that cannot answer that clearly is a risk to a fleet that grows in stages.

Spares, Lead Time and the Show

No manufacturer has a zero failure rate, and a rental buyer is rarely choosing between a perfect panel and an imperfect one. The choice is between two imperfect options with different resupply routes.

Three terms decide whether that route works. The lead time for a repeat order — we run 15 to 20 working days on standard production — the spare-parts arrangement agreed at purchase, and the warranty response behind it. A 3-year warranty means little if a failed panel takes months to replace, so the terms should be read together rather than separately.

Common Mistakes When Buying for a Fleet

Buying on pitch alone. A finer pitch is not automatically better for rental; it raises cost and may narrow the panel to fewer rigging configurations. The pitch should follow the audience distance of the typical job.

Mixing cabinet families. Two purchases from two product lines create two inventories. Standardising early is cheaper than reconciling later.

Ignoring the flight case and the road case. Panels spend more time in transit than on a wall. How they pack, stack and travel is part of the product decision.

Leaving spares to the warranty. A warranty replaces a failed panel eventually. A spare allows the show to go ahead tonight. The two are not the same service.

FAQ

Q: What should a rental company look for in an LED display manufacturer?
A: Four things matter more than peak specifications: how the panel survives handling and transport, whether the cabinets come from one standard platform so the fleet can share rigging and spares, how fast a replacement module can be delivered, and whether the manufacturer can support a fault that happens on site. A screen that is technically good but slow to resupply costs more across a season than one that is slightly plainer.
Q: Should a rental fleet standardise on one cabinet size?
A: It usually should. A single cabinet platform means one rigging method, one spare-parts set and one training standard. Where a product family offers 500×500mm and 500×1000mm cabinets that share locking and power details, a fleet can cover small and large walls without running two inventory systems.
Q: How many spare panels should a rental fleet carry?
A: The proportion depends on how hard the fleet is used, but the number that matters is the one that lets a show go ahead after a failure. Typically a fleet carries a small percentage of panels as immediate spares plus a defined route to a fast replacement. The manufacturer's lead time and spare-parts terms decide whether that route exists.
Q: Is COB better than SMD for rental?
A: Where panels are handled, stacked and flown repeatedly, a sealed COB face resists the most common damage — knocks to the lamp surface. Where the fleet is well cased and the panels are protected in transit, a conventional SMD module can be the more economical choice. The decision follows the handling pattern rather than the technology alone.
