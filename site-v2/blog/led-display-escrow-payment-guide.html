Escrow and Payment Practice for US Buyers

North American buyers rarely use escrow the same way an overseas buyer does, because the payment instruments available on each side are different. US buyers typically work through a domestic bank, and that changes what is practical.

Three structures appear repeatedly on US orders. The first is a straightforward wire transfer against a proforma invoice, used when the buyer has already qualified the supplier and the order value does not justify the cost of an escrow account. The second is a letter of credit, issued by the buyer's bank and confirmed through a bank the seller can access, which suits larger orders where neither side wants to carry the performance risk. The third is escrow, normally reserved for first orders with a new supplier or for orders large enough that the escrow fee is a small percentage of the value.

If you use an escrow agent, verify two things before transferring funds. First, that the agent is a regulated or licensed escrow provider in the state where the agreement is written, not an informal third party. Second, that the escrow agreement names the release conditions in a way that can be measured — an inspection sign-off, a bill of lading, or a named third-party inspection — rather than a general statement of satisfactory delivery.

One US-specific point: the escrow fee and the bank fees are usually borne by the buyer, so they should be budgeted at the quotation stage rather than discovered at payment. Ask the supplier to state on the proforma invoice which bank charges, if any, are theirs.
