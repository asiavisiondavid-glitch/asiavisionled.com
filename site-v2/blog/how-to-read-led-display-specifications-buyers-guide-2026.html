What are LED display specifications? LED display specifications are the written performance commitments a manufacturer makes about the product: pixel pitch, resolution, brightness, refresh rate, cabinet dimensions, power, and protection rating. A buyer who can read LED display specifications accurately can compare quotes, prevent downgraded components, and avoid a screen that fails its environment. This guide explains each field in plain language for 2026 purchasing decisions.

Why Specifications Decide the Quote

The same visible screen area can be quoted at very different prices because the specification underneath is different. A P3.9 outdoor screen at 6,000 nits is not the same product as a P4.8 screen at 5,000 nits, even if both are called a "6 by 3 meter LED wall". The price gap between those two configurations can reach 20-30%, which is why every quote must be read against the written specification, not against a marketing description.

In our project reviews, the most common source of disputes is a changed specification after the price is agreed. The model number and the specification sheet should be attached to the quotation, and any change should require a new quote. Buyers who enforce this simple rule avoid most renegotiation problems.

Pixel Pitch and Resolution

Pixel pitch is the distance from the center of one LED pixel to the next, measured in millimeters. Smaller pitch means more pixels in the same area and a shorter minimum viewing distance. The practical rule is that viewing distance in meters equals roughly 1-2 times the pitch in millimeters: a P2.6 display reads well from 3-6 meters, a P3.9 from 5-8 meters, and a P6 from 8-12 meters.

Resolution is the result of screen size and pixel pitch. A 4m by 2.25m P2.6 screen has roughly 1,536 by 864 pixels, while the same area at P3.9 has about 1,024 by 576 pixels. When content includes small text or live video, resolution limits what the audience can actually read.

Buyers should also ask about brightness binning. Two screens with the same nominal nits can look different if the LED supplier allows wide brightness tolerances. A factory that documents LED brand and binning, such as Nationstar, Kinglight, or equivalent, gives you a more predictable screen.

Refresh Rate, Gray Scale, and Color Depth

Refresh rate is the number of times the screen redraws the image per second. A rate above 1,920 Hz is important for events, broadcast, and sports because cameras can otherwise capture dark bands. Gray scale, usually expressed as 14-16 bits, affects how smoothly colors transition in gradients. Color depth and color temperature control also matter for retail and corporate branding, where a screen must reproduce logos accurately.

Cabinet Design, IP Rating, and Power

Cabinet design determines serviceability. Die-cast aluminum cabinets are stronger and more accurate for rental, while fixed installations may use lighter sheet-metal designs. Check cabinet weight, front and rear service options, and whether the module design allows tool-less replacement. IP rating tells you dust and water resistance: outdoor displays normally need IP65 front protection and at least IP54 rear protection. Power consumption affects the electrical design of the venue, so request watts per square meter at peak and average values.

Minimum specification for a typical 2026 project:
✅ Pixel pitch matched to viewing distance
✅ Brightness matched to environment
✅ Refresh rate above 1,920 Hz for events or cameras
✅ IP65 front / IP54 rear for outdoor
✅ Documented LED brand and brightness binning
✅ Peak and average power per m2
✅ UL/ETL and FCC documentation for US projects

Red Flags in Specification Sheets

  • No model number or a generic "same as" description
  • Brightness listed as "up to" without a tested value
  • Component brands omitted or changed after order
  • Pixel pitch stated only as module pitch instead of cabinet pitch
  • Certification files for a different model family

When the specification sheet is clear, the rest of the buying process becomes faster. You can compare quotes on the same basis, verify certification with the file number in UL Product iQ, and hold the factory to the model it promised. Comparing Spec Sheets on the Same Basis

Specification sheets often use different test conditions, so the first step is to normalize the data. Confirm whether brightness is measured in nits, whether power is average or maximum, and whether refresh rate is quoted at the final drive setting. Check whether viewing angle refers to a contrast threshold or a brightness threshold. When the same terms are measured differently, a direct comparison can make one product appear stronger without any real performance advantage.

Ask each supplier to provide the complete cabinet specification, not only the module specification. Cabinet dimensions, weight, pixel configuration, power connectors, data connectors, and maintenance access all affect the installed result. A module may meet the desired pixel pitch while the cabinet design creates unnecessary seams, difficult service access, or a structural load that the venue cannot support.

Turning Specifications into Acceptance Criteria

A specification becomes useful when it is tied to a test. Define how brightness and color uniformity will be checked, which test patterns will be used, and what result will trigger correction. For camera-facing applications, include refresh rate, shutter speed, and gray-scale tests. For outdoor projects, add waterproofing, structural, and thermal checks. The supplier should agree to these tests before production rather than after the display arrives on site.

The purchase order should also define tolerances. Perfect uniformity is not a realistic requirement for every product class, but vague language such as "high quality" is equally unhelpful. State the maximum acceptable dead-pixel rate, brightness variation, cabinet gap, and calibration result. Clear tolerances protect both sides because they turn a subjective dispute into a measurable inspection item.

Checking Power, Weight, and Structural Data

Power and weight figures should be reviewed together with the intended mounting method. A lighter cabinet can reduce steel cost, but it may still require a rigid frame to maintain alignment. Maximum power helps size the electrical supply, while average power helps estimate operating cost. The buyer should confirm which figure was used for cable sizing and whether the supplier included derating for heat or long cable runs.

For rental systems, compare cabinet weight, hanging hardware, flight-case capacity, and the labor needed to build a standard wall. A small weight difference can become significant when a crew moves hundreds of panels during a tour. For fixed installations, compare service clearance and access to the rear of the screen. Easy module removal can reduce maintenance cost even when the initial hardware specification is similar.

Warranty Language and Component Traceability

Read the warranty section as carefully as the performance table. Confirm the warranty period for modules, power supplies, receiving cards, controllers, and mechanical parts. Check whether coverage includes parts only or parts and labor, and whether shipping costs are shared. Exclusions for water damage, power surges, unauthorized repair, or improper installation should be understood before the order is placed.

Request a component list and serial-number record for the production batch. Traceability helps the supplier diagnose a fault and verify that replacement parts match the original build. It also supports warranty claims when a component fails earlier than expected. A specification package is stronger when the promised performance, the approved components, and the acceptance test are all connected to the same model and batch.

FAQ

Q: What is the most important LED display specification?
A: There is no single number. Pixel pitch, brightness, refresh rate, cabinet design, and IP rating work together. For a project, the most important specification is the one that matches the viewing distance and environment.
Q: How do I choose the right pixel pitch?
A: Use the viewing distance rule: minimum viewing distance in meters is roughly 1-2 times the pixel pitch in millimeters. A P2.6 display suits 3-6 meters, while P4 or P6 suits 10-30 meters.
Q: How many nits do I need for an outdoor LED display?
A: Outdoor displays in direct sunlight normally need 5,000-7,000 nits. Semi-outdoor and shaded areas can use 3,500-4,500 nits, and indoor displays usually need 800-1,200 nits.
Q: What does refresh rate mean for LED displays?
A: Refresh rate is how often the image is redrawn per second, measured in Hertz. A rate above 1,920 Hz reduces flicker in cameras and is recommended for broadcast, sports, and rental productions.
Q: What is IP rating and why does it matter?
A: IP rating describes protection against dust and water. Outdoor LED displays should normally be rated IP65 for the front and at least IP54 for the rear; indoor displays have lower requirements.
