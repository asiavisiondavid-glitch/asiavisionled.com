What does it take to build an LED backdrop for a global product launch? This project put an 80 square metre curved LED wall behind the stage at a Shenzhen technology launch — P2.6 rental panels, 7680Hz refresh for the live stream, and wings that curved forward on each side. It had to look cinematic to 300 people in the room and simultaneously survive broadcast cameras sending the feed to hundreds of thousands online.

That double requirement — a stage that works for the audience and for the lens — is what separates a launch backdrop from an ordinary event screen. Here is how the specification was put together and what a buyer planning a similar build should know.

The Brief: One Screen, Two Audiences

A product launch has an unusual constraint. The people in the room see the wall with their own eyes, but the far larger audience sees it only through a camera. A wall that looks perfect in person can read as flickering bars on the live stream, and a wall tuned only for camera can look washed out on stage.

Three requirements came out of that brief:

  • Scale — the backdrop had to fill the stage so the presenter reads as standing inside a visual, not in front of a PowerPoint.
  • Camera safety — no scan lines, no banding, no moire in the broadcast feed.
  • Shape — a flat wall would have looked like a billboard. The design called for wings curving forward on both sides to wrap the stage.

Why P2.6 for an 80㎡ Stage Backdrop

Pixel pitch is chosen by the closest viewer, and on a launch stage the closest viewer is a camera.

At roughly 80 square metres, the wall is large enough that the audience sits well back, but a broadcast camera can push in far closer than any person in the room. The question is not "how far is the back row" but "how tight will the tightest shot be."

P2.6 was the balance point: dense enough that the pixels disappear at stage distance and in medium shots, without paying for fine pitch across 80 square metres of wall that no one views from a metre away. The panels used here are from the COB Z rental series, which covers P1.95 and P2.6 in the same cabinet family — so a project can step up to the finer pitch in one section without changing rigging or spares.

7680Hz: The Number That Decides Broadcast Quality

This is the specification most launch projects get wrong, because it does not affect how the wall looks to the eye at all.

A camera does not capture a moment — it captures a slice of time set by its shutter. If the LED panel refreshes more slowly than the camera samples, the camera catches the panel mid-cycle, and you see the result as horizontal scan lines, rolling bands, or a shimmering moire pattern. The audience in the room sees none of it; the stream sees all of it.

The rule of thumb: for broadcast and live stream work, the panel refresh rate needs to be several times the camera frame rate before artifacts disappear. Consumer broadcast sit at 50/60Hz frame rates, high-speed and XR cameras run faster still. 3840Hz is enough for the eye and for casual filming; 7680Hz is what makes a screen safe for professional live production.

The COB Z panels used on this stage run at 7680Hz. In practice that means the camera can be pushed in on the performer against the backdrop, or pan across the wall during a walk-on, without the wall betraying that it is a screen at all. For a launch event where the backdrop is the visual identity of the announcement, that is not a luxury specification — it is the whole point.

The Curved Wings: Where Rental Cabinets Earn Their Keep

The design called for the wall to break forward at both ends, wrapping the stage rather than sitting behind it as a rectangle.

This is routine for rental cabinets and awkward for fixed ones. Rental panels lock edge to edge with adjustable angle joiners, so a run of flat wall can transition into a curve at any cabinet seam. The practical limit is the cabinet width: at 500mm per cabinet, an arc stays visually smooth down to a fairly tight radius before the seam gap starts to show, and the content team can mask the remaining gap in the artwork.

The same cabinets that make the curve possible also make the schedule possible. Rental frames arrive pre-rigged, hang from a single flown truss line, and are levelled cabinet by cabinet — which is why a wall of this size can be up in a day rather than a week.

Why Service Access Is Not a Detail

On a launch there is no second take. If a panel goes down during rehearsal — a connector, a receiving card, a power supply — the crew has to reach it without dismantling the wall or losing the day's camera session.

Front-and-rear serviceable cabinets mean any single panel can be swapped from the front while the wall stays flown. On a fixed installation this is convenience; on a launch with a locked rehearsal schedule it is the difference between a fix and a crisis.

Delivery and Build Sequence

Large event walls are won or lost in the schedule, not the hardware. The sequence that keeps a launch on track:

  1. Rigging points confirmed early — the flown truss layout is decided with the venue before the wall is finalised, not after.
  2. Panels pre-built and pre-tested — cabinets are assembled and run in the workshop so the venue time is spent hanging, not fault-finding.
  3. Fly and level in one day — the wall goes up, seams are aligned, and the curve is set.
  4. Content and camera rehearsal the next day — playback runs against the actual cameras, at the actual shutter, so any refresh or brightness issue surfaces while there is still time to fix it.

The last step is the one that catches problems. A wall can pass every bench test and still interact badly with a specific camera at a specific shutter angle. Testing with the real broadcast kit on site, before the event, is what turns a 7680Hz specification into a clean feed.

The Result

The wall ran as an 80 square metre curved backdrop behind the launch stage, carrying the event's visual identity to 300+ people in the room and to the live stream audience of hundreds of thousands — with no refresh artifacts reported in the broadcast feed.

For the client, the outcome is not just "the screen worked." It is that the backdrop read as part of the show on camera: the presenter stood inside the visual, close-ups held up, and the wall never betrayed that it was an assembly of 500mm panels.

What to Take Away If You Are Planning a Launch Wall

  • Choose pitch by camera distance, not audience distance. The tightest planned shot sets the requirement.
  • 7680Hz is the broadcast number. If there is a camera and a live audience, treat high refresh as mandatory, not optional.
  • Rental cabinets are what make curved stages practical. Angle-adjustable joins turn a flat wall into a wrap.
  • Insist on front service. On a locked rehearsal schedule, reachable panels are risk management.
  • Test with the real cameras before the event. A refresh specification is only proven against the broadcast kit it will meet.

FAQ

Q: Why does a product launch LED backdrop need 7680Hz refresh?
A: Broadcast and live-stream cameras sample the screen at their own shutter speed. If the LED refresh rate is too low relative to the shutter, the camera captures scan lines, banding or moire instead of a clean image. 7680Hz gives the camera enough samples per frame that the panel reads as steady light on stream.
Q: What pixel pitch is right for a launch stage backdrop?
A: It depends on the closest camera and audience distance. For a large stage backdrop of roughly 80 square metres viewed from 5m or more, P2.6 gives dense enough pixels for camera close-ups without the cost of a finer pitch. Closer or tighter shots push you toward P1.95 or finer.
Q: Can rental LED panels be built into a curved wall?
A: Yes. Rental cabinets are joined with angle adjusters on the locking edges, so a flat wall can curve outward into wings on each side. The limit is the cabinet width: a 500mm cabinet can form a smooth arc down to a fairly tight radius before the seam gap becomes visible.
Q: How long does an 80 square metre stage backdrop take to build?
A: With pre-rigged frames and a trained crew, an 80 square metre rental wall is typically flown and levelled in a single working day, leaving the next day for content playback testing and camera rehearsal before the event.
