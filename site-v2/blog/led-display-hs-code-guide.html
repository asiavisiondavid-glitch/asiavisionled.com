There is no single LED display HS code or LED screen HS code that every customs authority will accept for every shipment. The practical way to find the heading is to describe the goods as they will actually be shipped — a complete video wall, a cabinet, or a module on its own — then match that description to the destination country's current official tariff schedule. A licensed customs broker should confirm the heading and the national digits before the first entry is filed. Do not copy a code from a previous invoice, a freight quote, or a supplier packing list without that check.

This guide walks through that process, then explains why a complete cabinet or system and a spare module can fall under different headings. It is written for importers and exporters. It is not a classification ruling, and it does not replace advice from a licensed broker in the country of entry.

How to Find the LED Display HS Code

Use this sequence for an LED display, LED screen, or LED display module. The aim is a documented heading you can hand to a broker, not a guess from memory.

  1. Write a precise product description: what the article is, what it does, how it is presented (assembled wall, unassembled cabinets, or loose modules), and what is packed with it (processor, receiving cards, power supplies, cables, steel frame).
  2. Open the official tariff schedule of the destination country, not a blog table or a freight-forwarder spreadsheet. For a United States entry, that schedule is the Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States. For a United Kingdom entry, it is the UK Integrated Online Tariff. Other markets publish their own extensions of the Harmonized System.
  3. Identify the four-digit heading that best matches the goods, using the General Rules of Interpretation and the section and chapter notes in that schedule. Two headings come up often for this product family: monitors and projectors (heading 8528) and electric sound or visual signalling apparatus, including indicator panels (heading 8531). Parts of apparatus under headings 8524 to 8528 are grouped under heading 8529. Those heading texts are published in the official schedules cited at the end of this article.
  4. Read down to the six-digit international subheading and then to the eight- or ten-digit national code that actually carries the duty rate. National digits are not interchangeable between countries.
  5. Ask a licensed customs broker to confirm the heading and the national code for that exact description and destination. For a first shipment, a high-value order, or a product that could fit more than one heading, consider requesting a binding classification ruling from the customs authority.

Invoice and packing-list wording should match the description used for classification. A vague line such as “LED screen” or “display parts” makes the heading harder to defend if customs asks for evidence.

What the HS Code Is

The Harmonized System is the international classification of traded goods maintained by the World Customs Organization. Countries build their national tariffs on the same six-digit core, then add digits for national rates and statistical breaks. A code that is correct at six digits can still be wrong at the national level if the last digits are taken from another country.

LevelDigitsMeaning
Chapter2Broad category
Heading4Product group
Subheading6International detail, common across HS users
National code8–10Country-specific rate and statistical break

The system is updated periodically, and national schedules are revised more often than that. A code used on an older shipment should be re-checked against the current schedule before it is reused.

Cabinet vs Module Tariff Differences

This is the distinction buyers search for when they ask about an LED display module HS code as well as the code for a complete LED display or LED screen. The heading can change with how the goods are presented, not only with the brand or pixel pitch.

A complete modular LED video wall, presented together with the video processing system that lets it display video from ordinary signal sources, has been treated in official EU classification regulations as a functional unit under heading 8528 (monitors), and not under heading 8531 (indicator panels). Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 103/2012 classified a described “LED wall” of that kind under the Combined Nomenclature, and Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2015/712 later amended the eight-digit CN code after the nomenclature itself was restructured. The 2015 amendment also applied the rule for incomplete or unassembled goods to a wall presented unassembled. Those regulations describe a specific product (including a coarse 24 mm pitch and a supplied processor). They are official illustrations of the heading-level logic; they are not a standing worldwide code for every indoor or outdoor cabinet sold today.

A module or “LED tile” shipped on its own is a different article. Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) No 875/2014 classified a described LED tile — an aluminium housing with LEDs, power and data connectors, and mounting brackets, presented for a modular video wall, but without a video processor and unable to display video from a source by itself — as a part under heading 8529, not as a monitor under 8528 and not as a visual signalling apparatus under 8531. That is why a spare-module shipment should not automatically use the same heading as a complete wall.

The official eight-digit CN codes named in those 2012–2015 regulations (including 8528 59 80, later 8528 59 39, and 8529 90 92) have been revised as the Combined Nomenclature changed. Do not treat those historical eight-digit numbers as the current code for a 2026 shipment in the EU or anywhere else. Use the heading-level reasoning, then read the current official schedule and confirm the national digits with a licensed broker.

Why the Code Matters

The classified code decides the duty rate and can also decide licensing, standards, or trade-remedy measures such as anti-dumping duty. A wrong code means the wrong duty, a delayed entry, or a later assessment. Because duty is often part of the tax base, an error compounds in the landed cost.

The rate itself lives on the national schedule and can change. Re-check it for the destination before each order rather than carrying forward a rate from a previous project. Our customs clearance guide covers the entry process that follows once the code is confirmed.

Confirm the Code with a Licensed Broker

A licensed customs broker in the country of entry should confirm the code for the product and the destination. For a complex or high-value product, a classification ruling requested from the authority in advance gives a written decision that binds that authority for the goods described. The importer of record remains responsible for the declaration even if a supplier or forwarder suggested a code.

The product description on the invoice should match the description used in the classification file. If the product changes between shipments — a different pitch, a processor included or omitted, modules shipped without cabinets — the file should be updated deliberately rather than left to drift.

Common HS Code Mistakes

  • Guessing the LED display HS code or copying one from a different product
  • Using a complete-wall code for a module-only shipment, or the reverse
  • Declaring mixed contents of a container under a single heading
  • Copying an eight-digit national code from another country or from an outdated regulation
  • Leaving a vague invoice description that cannot support the heading
  • Failing to check trade-remedy measures attached to the chosen code
  • Skipping a licensed broker on the first entry into a new market

Each of these can produce the wrong duty or a penalty. The remedy is a current official schedule, a precise description, and written broker confirmation.

Confirming the Code for a US Entry

For an import into the United States, the classification is declared on the entry and becomes the basis on which duty is assessed, so confirming it is part of purchasing rather than an administrative step afterwards.

Three points distinguish US practice. First, the HTSUS extends beyond the international six-digit level, so the national subdivisions are what the entry is actually made under. Second, an importer who is uncertain can request a binding ruling from US Customs and Border Protection before importing, which fixes the treatment for the goods described. Third, classification is tied to the importer of record, because that is the party liable for the duty and responsible for the records.

Where a code appears to change between shipments, that is what draws attention. The code on each entry should match the classification file, and where the product has genuinely changed the file should be updated. Confirm the code at the time of each order with the customs broker handling the entry, rather than carrying it forward from a previous project.

Keeping the Classification Record

Keep the classification record with the shipment file: the product description, the heading and national code used, the extract from the official schedule, the reasoning, and any broker advice or ruling. The same product should carry the same code on later shipments unless the goods or the schedule have changed. A documented classification is easier to defend in an audit than a number remembered from a previous job.

FAQ

Q: What is the LED display HS code or LED screen HS code?
A: There is no single worldwide number that every customs authority will accept for every LED display or LED screen. Start with the destination country's official tariff schedule, identify the heading that matches the goods as they are shipped, and have a licensed customs broker confirm the heading and the national digits before the first entry is filed.
Q: How do I find the heading for an LED display?
A: Describe the goods precisely (complete video wall, cabinet, or module), open the current official tariff schedule for the destination, match the product to a heading using the General Rules of Interpretation, then read the national subheadings and any chapter notes. A licensed customs broker should confirm the result.
Q: Is the HS code for an LED display module the same as for a complete cabinet?
A: Not necessarily. A complete LED video wall presented with its processing system can fall under a different heading from a module or tile shipped on its own as a part. Official EU classification regulations have treated those two presentations differently. Classify each invoice line separately and confirm both with a broker.
Q: Can I use one code for a mixed shipment of cabinets, modules and cables?
A: No. Cabinets, spare modules, power supplies, control cards, cables and steel structures can each require their own heading. Declaring the whole container under one description is a common reason for a query or a reassessment.
Q: Does the HS code decide the duty rate?
A: Yes. The duty rate, and often licensing or trade-remedy measures, follow the classified code in the destination schedule. Rates differ by country and can change, so re-check the rate for each order rather than copying a previous shipment.
