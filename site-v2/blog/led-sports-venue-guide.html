What Makes Sports Venue LED Different From Advertising Screens

A sports venue LED display is judged by a different standard than an advertising screen. A roadside billboard succeeds if it looks vivid from a passing car. A stadium screen succeeds only when a referee on the field, a spectator in row 40, and a broadcast camera fifty meters away all read the same number at the same instant. That three-way requirement drives every specification decision on the project.

Scoreboards must stay legible in direct sunlight, resist the strobing that ruins slow-motion replays, and update a game clock without visible lag. Perimeter and ribbon boards face the opposite problem: they sit at eye level behind players, so they must register clearly on camera without flaring into the lens or distracting the athletes standing directly in front of them.

Venues also run on a fixed calendar. A football stadium cannot close for a two-week screen upgrade in the middle of a season, and a multi-purpose arena may host basketball, ice hockey, and a concert within the same week. Maintenance access, cabinet weight, and control-system flexibility end up mattering as much as raw image quality, because one screen has to serve several sports without a rebuild between events.

Finally, sports LED is a safety and revenue asset at the same time. The same surface carries the score, the sponsor loop, the instant replay, and the emergency message. Buyers who plan for all four roles from the start avoid the common mistake of installing a beautiful scoreboard that cannot be repurposed for sponsorship or crowd messaging.

The Four Screen Zones in a Modern Venue

The phrase stadium screen hides four distinct products, each with its own geometry and technical brief. Specifying them separately is the fastest way to control cost and to make sure every surface does its job.

Main scoreboard. Usually a large-format screen or a cluster of screens hung at one or both ends of the pitch. It carries the score, the clock, player statistics, and instant replay. Pitch is typically P6 to P10, because the nearest spectator still sits fifteen to forty meters away and the content is mostly large numerals and video rather than fine text.

Perimeter LED. The low boards that ring the field at roughly 0.8 to 1 meter high. These exist mainly for broadcast sponsorship, so they are specified for camera performance rather than for the crowd. Pitch around P10 to P20 is normal, and panel height is chosen so the boards do not block the first rows of seating.

Ribbon boards. Long, narrow strips mounted on the fascias between seating tiers. They show rolling text, scores from other games, and sponsor rotation. Because they are seen at a sharp angle, wide viewing angle and uniform colour along the whole strip matter more than peak brightness.

Hospitality, concourse and entry screens. The smallest and most numerous category. These sit closer to retail digital signage, at P2.5 to P4, and they are usually the cheapest place to start if a venue wants to test the water before committing to a full scoreboard rebuild.

Refresh Rate, Camera Sync and the Moire Trap

Refresh rate is the single specification that separates a venue screen from a wall of the same size in a shopping centre. A camera does not capture a continuous image; it samples the screen in short slices. If the LED refresh rate sits close to the camera shutter speed, horizontal bands roll across the picture on the broadcast feed even though the screen looks perfect to the naked eye.

For indoor arenas we specify at least 3840 Hz, and for outdoor venues at least 1920 Hz, treating those numbers as a floor rather than a target. Where several cameras shoot the same screen at different shutter angles, a higher refresh rate gives the broadcast engineer more room to find an angle that is clean for all of them. Genlock, which locks the screen timing to the house reference signal, removes the last source of drift between the display and the production switcher.

The second camera problem is moire. On a fine-pitch screen the regular grid of LED pixels can beat against the regular grid of the camera sensor and produce a shimmering pattern over the picture. This gets worse as pitch gets finer, which is why a P10 perimeter board is often easier on camera than a P2.6 concourse wall. A high fill factor and a matte, low-glare surface treatment suppress the effect, and a slight defocus on the camera side handles what remains.

Grayscale depth matters just as much under stadium lighting. A screen with low bit-depth processing bands badly across a dark sky or a shadowed stand, and that banding is more visible on camera than in the stadium. Fourteen-bit or higher processing keeps gradients smooth across the whole brightness range, which is what makes a replay look like television rather than a computer monitor.

Structure, Weather Protection and Control Room Integration

A stadium screen is a building attachment with a public-safety role, so wind load and seismic restraint usually govern the steel design. A centre-hung arena scoreboard hangs from the roof structure and must be analysed as a suspended mass that sways during a concert; a perimeter board sits on the ground or on a low plinth and must survive being struck by players, balls, and ground staff. A structural engineer should sign off on the frame, the anchor points, and every penetration into the building.

Outdoor venues need genuine weather protection. Cabinets should carry IP65 front and rear, with sealed signal paths and drainage for condensation. In coastal or high-humidity markets, add conformal coating on the driver boards and stainless fasteners, because salt air attacks the parts that are hardest to reach. Thermal design follows the same logic: a scoreboard often runs near full brightness for hours in the middle of summer, so rear plenums and derating sensors protect both the LEDs and the power supplies.

Control room integration is where venue projects are won or lost. The screen must accept feeds from the timing system, the replay server, the sponsorship playlist, and the public-address or emergency system, and it must switch between them without a visible glitch. That usually means a media server or a switcher with preset scenes rather than a bare video processor, plus a redundancy path so a failed source does not blank the match clock.

Service access has to be designed in. Front-serviceable cabinets let a technician pull a module from the face of the screen, which suits boards mounted tight against a stand. Rear-serviceable cabinets need a walkable plenum behind the display. On a centre-hung scoreboard, plan a lift or catwalk route because the power and control cabinets are the parts most likely to need attention during a season.

FAQ: Buyer Questions

Q: What pixel pitch does a stadium scoreboard need?
A: P6 to P10 suits most main scoreboards viewed from fifteen to sixty meters. Use P10 to P20 for ground-level perimeter boards whose main audience is the broadcast camera rather than the crowd.
Q: Why does my LED screen flicker on television?
A: The refresh rate is too close to the camera shutter speed. Raising the screen to 3840 Hz indoors or 1920 Hz outdoors and locking it with genlock removes the rolling bands from the broadcast feed.
Q: Can one screen handle both scoring and advertising?
A: Yes, with a media server that stores preset scenes for score, replay, sponsorship, and emergency messaging. The scenes switch without a glitch so the clock is never interrupted mid-game.
Q: How long does a venue screen installation take?
A: From the point the structure is ready, a typical scoreboard or perimeter install runs two to five weeks on site. Asia Vision ships production orders in 15 to 20 working days, but stadium access windows usually set the real calendar.
Q: What weather rating do outdoor boards need?
A: Specify IP65 front and rear for outdoor perimeter and facade screens. Add conformal coating and stainless fasteners in coastal venues, where salt air corrodes unprotected driver boards.
Q: How many spare parts should a venue keep?
A: Hold at least two to three percent of modules as spares plus one spare power supply and one receiving card per screen, so a single failure can be fixed between fixtures without waiting on a shipment.
