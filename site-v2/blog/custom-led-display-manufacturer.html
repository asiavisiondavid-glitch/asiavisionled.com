"Custom" is the most overused word in LED sales. It can mean a genuinely new cabinet designed around an architect's opening, or it can mean a standard panel with a different sticker on the controller. Both get called custom, and the price difference between them is large — which is why a buyer should establish which one is being quoted before comparing offers.

This page sets out what a factory can actually customise, what each layer costs in time and money, and how to tell a real custom project from a re-badged standard one.

The Four Layers of "Custom"

Custom work is not one thing. In practice it falls into four areas, and they carry very different implications.

LayerWhat changesCost and lead-time impact
Cabinet dimensionsA new enclosure size to fit a specific openingHighest — design, tooling, prototype and verification
Pixel pitchA different module inside an existing cabinetModerate — a module change, not a new enclosure
ShapeCurved, cornered or irregular geometryDepends on whether the cabinet already supports the curve
Finish and housingColour, cladding, integration with the surrounding finishLower — cosmetic and coordination, not structural

Most projects need one or two of these, not all four. A quotation that treats everything as fully custom is either over-engineering the job or hiding a standard product behind the word.

Layer One: Cabinet Dimensions

A different cabinet size is the deepest form of customisation. It means new drawings, a prototype, a structural review of how the panel carries load, and a test of how the modules sit in the new frame.

This is where the real cost sits, and it is a cost that does not disappear when only one unit is ordered. A custom cabinet has to be designed and verified whether the order is one screen or fifty, which is why small custom projects look expensive per square metre and large ones look reasonable.

The credible way to quote this is to supply dimensioned drawings and a prototype before production, so the buyer can check the opening, the mounting and the finish against the real room. A quote without those documents is a promise rather than a specification.

Layer Two: Pitch in an Existing Cabinet

Changing the module inside an existing enclosure is a much lighter form of customisation, and it is often what a project actually needs.

Where a product family shares a cabinet platform across several pitches — our COB EF series, for example, takes P1.5625 and P1.953 modules in the same 500×500mm frame — the buyer can match the pitch to the viewing distance without a new enclosure. The cabinet, the mounting and the service method stay the same; only the module changes.

The question that separates these two layers: "Does this pitch need a new cabinet, or does it fit the standard one?" A supplier that can answer with a drawing has answered it. A supplier that answers "yes, custom" without a drawing has not.

Layer Three: Shape

Curved, cornered and irregular walls sit between the two extremes. Where the standard cabinet already supports the geometry — a rental cabinet that curves to ±5°, for example — no new tooling is needed and the project is close to standard.

Where the shape falls outside the cabinet's mechanical limits, the enclosure has to be designed around it, and the project moves into layer-one territory. The dividing line is the cabinet's rated curve, not the designer's intention, so it is worth establishing early rather than at approval stage.

Layer Four: Finish and Housing

The outermost layer is appearance: cladding, colour, how the screen meets the surrounding wall, and whether the enclosure is visible or concealed.

This is where coordination matters more than tooling. A recessed screen needs a reveal detail agreed with the interior contractor; a flush wall needs the substrate and the screen depth settled together. Getting these wrong does not break the display, but it does leave a visibly unresolved junction that the client will notice for the life of the installation.

How a Custom Project Should Run

A custom LED project has a sequence, and skipping it is what turns a custom order into a dispute.

Brief and drawings. The opening, the viewing distances and the finish are captured and turned into dimensioned drawings. Prototype or sample. Where a new cabinet is involved, a unit is built and checked against the drawings. Written test criteria. The buyer and factory agree what "pass" means before production. Production and inspection. The batch is checked against those criteria before shipping. Delivery and commissioning. The screen is installed and verified on site.

Each step produces a document. A project with documents can be managed; a project without them depends on goodwill.

What a Factory Cannot Change

Honesty about limits is part of the service, because a promise that cannot be kept costs more than a refusal.

Some things cannot be made custom at reasonable cost or in a reasonable time: a cabinet that would not carry its own load, a pitch that no supplier produces, a curve that exceeds the mechanical limit of the module, or a finish that conflicts with the sealing the screen needs. In each case the right answer is to change the design, not to accept an order that cannot be delivered.

FAQ

Q: What does a custom LED display actually include?
A: In practice custom work falls into four areas: cabinet dimensions, pixel pitch, the shape of the wall (flat, curved, cornered or irregular), and the finish or housing around the screen. Each has a different cost implication and a different lead time, and a project rarely needs all four.
Q: Is a custom LED display more expensive?
A: Usually, because custom work changes tooling, production runs and testing rather than only the module inside the cabinet. A custom cabinet has to be designed, prototyped and verified, and that cost sits inside the project whether it is spread across one unit or fifty. Projects that only need a different pitch or a standard cabinet in a different finish carry far less of that cost.
Q: How long does a custom LED project take?
A: It depends on which layer is being customised. A different module in an existing cabinet is close to standard lead time. A new cabinet requires design, prototype and approval before production, which adds a development stage ahead of manufacturing. The schedule should be agreed at quotation rather than assumed.
Q: Can any factory do custom work?
A: Many can quote it; fewer can design and verify it. The difference shows in whether the supplier can produce drawings, a prototype and written test criteria before the order, or only an assurance that it will be handled. A custom project without those documents is a custom project without a specification.
