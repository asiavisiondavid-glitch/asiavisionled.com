A display is only as good as the modules inside it, and yet module selection is often treated as a formality once the pixel pitch has been chosen. In practice the module package, the driver arrangement and the brightness binning decide how the screen looks on camera, how it behaves when a customer stands six feet away, and whether a replacement part ordered three years later still matches the wall it has to blend into.

Module choice also interacts with everything downstream. A sealed package suits a humid or public environment but constrains the servicing approach; a finer pitch raises the data and power demand per cabinet; a different brightness bin shows up as a visible patch on an otherwise uniform wall. This guide covers what to weigh when selecting modules, the practices that keep a wall consistent over its life, and the specification and support questions that come up in procurement.

What Are the Key Considerations?

The first consideration is the module package and what it means for the environment the screen will live in. A conventional surface-mount package exposes individual emitters and offers strong optical performance with straightforward servicing, but it is the most vulnerable to physical knocks and to moisture if the cabinet is not adequately protected. A chip-on-board package seals the emitter array behind a continuous surface, which raises contrast, resists accidental contact and simplifies cleaning, at a higher cost per unit area that is justified by fine pitch and close viewing. A glue-on-board package sits between the two, adding a protective layer over a surface-mount array for installations where impact resistance is the priority.

The second consideration is pitch against viewing distance, and here the discipline is to resist overspecifying. Resolution beyond what an audience can perceive is capital that never returns, so pitch should be chosen from the shortest realistic viewing distance rather than from the finest pitch the budget allows. The relationship is straightforward: the closer the closest viewer, the finer the pitch needs to be. A wall read from ten metres is well served by a pitch that would look coarse to someone standing two metres away, and the same money spent on pitch instead of on brightness or redundancy often buys a better outcome.

The third consideration is consistency, because a video wall is judged as a single surface rather than as a collection of cabinets. Modules from different production runs can differ slightly in brightness and colour, and a wall assembled from mixed bins shows seams that are invisible in isolation but obvious once the screen is running a pale, uniform image. Selecting a package with controlled binning and ordering the whole wall from one batch is the practical defence, and the same discipline has to be applied to any replacement parts ordered later in the screen's life.

The fourth consideration is serviceability and the spare parts plan. A module is the most commonly replaced component in a display, so the selection decision should include how a module is removed, whether that can be done from the front, and whether the same part will still be available in three or five years. What Are the Best Practices?

Order the whole wall from a single production batch. Binning exists because LED emitters vary slightly, and the variation is invisible in a single cabinet but visible across a large wall running a uniform test pattern. Placing the entire screen as one order, rather than adding cabinets later from a different run, is the most effective way to guarantee uniformity. Where an expansion is planned, it is worth telling the supplier at the original order so that the batch size accounts for the future requirement and the binning is consistent between the two deliveries.

Verify the module in the environment it will run in. Brightness and contrast read very differently in a showroom than in a glazed lobby or an outdoor canopy, and a demonstration in the wrong conditions produces a selection that disappoints on site. Where possible, review the module running content that resembles the final use case rather than a saturated demo reel, because pale, uniform content is where uneven binning and low contrast become obvious, while a colourful promotional clip conceals exactly the faults a buyer should be looking for.

Plan the spare parts allocation at the same time as the order. A small reserve of modules held centrally or on site converts a failure from an outage into a routine repair, and because production is quoted at 15 to 20 working days, a screen with no spares can be dark for a meaningful period while a replacement is made. The reserve should be sized to the cabinet count and the maintenance round, and it should come from the same batch as the wall so that any panel fitted later matches the rest of the screen.

Document the module specification and keep it with the installation. Three years after commissioning, the person who chose the module may have moved on, and a replacement decision made without the original specification risks a mismatch in brightness bin, driver behaviour or connector type. An asset record listing the module part number, the pitch, the batch reference and the supplier contact is a small administrative step that protects a substantial capital asset.

FAQ: Buyer Questions

Q: What is the difference between SMD, COB and GOB LED modules?
A: Surface mount devices expose individual emitters on the module face, which gives strong optical performance and straightforward repair but leaves the emitters vulnerable to impact and to moisture if the cabinet is not well sealed. Chip on board seals the emitter array behind a continuous surface, raising contrast, protecting the array from contact and simplifying cleaning, at a higher unit cost that is justified for fine pitch and close-viewing installations. Glue on board adds a protective layer over a surface-mount array, which suits environments where physical impact is the dominant risk and a moderate cost premium is acceptable.
Q: Which pixel pitch should I choose for my viewing distance?
A: Choose the coarsest pitch that your closest realistic viewer cannot fault, rather than the finest pitch your budget allows. Pitch and viewing distance are directly related, so a wall whose nearest audience stands two metres needs a finer pitch than one read from ten metres, and spending on resolution beyond perception returns nothing. For most commercial interior walls the practical band is P1.9 to P2.6, while close-view corporate and control-room installations move to P1.2 to P1.5 and large-format outdoor or long-distance walls sit at P3.9 and above.
Q: How do I make sure replacement modules match the original wall?
A: Order replacement modules from the same production batch as the original screen wherever possible, and record the batch reference with the installation documentation. Modules from a different run can differ slightly in brightness and colour, and the difference is invisible on a saturated promotional clip but obvious on a pale, uniform image, which is exactly the content a corporate or retail wall spends much of its time showing. If the original batch is no longer available, the supplier may be able to bin-match a replacement to the wall's specification, which is worth requesting before ordering.
Q: Does a sealed COB module make maintenance harder?
A: It changes the maintenance method rather than making it harder. A sealed face cannot be repaired at the emitter level in the way an exposed array can, so a fault on a chip-on-board module is resolved by replacing the module rather than by repairing individual lamps. Against that, the sealed surface is more resistant to cleaning, dust and accidental contact, so it typically needs less intervention in the first place. In a public or humid environment that trade is usually favourable, and front-service mounting keeps the replacement procedure straightforward.
Q: What brightness variation is acceptable across a video wall?
A: Any variation that a viewer can see on a uniform image is too much, because a video wall is read as one continuous surface rather than as separate cabinets. The practical control is not a tolerance figure but a process: order the whole wall from a single production batch, verify it with pale uniform test content at the commissioning stage, and hold any spare modules from the same batch. A wall that looks uniform on a promotional clip can still reveal slight differences on a white or light grey image, so the acceptance test should use content that exposes the problem rather than content that hides it.
Q: How long should a spare module last in storage?
A: Store spare modules in the original packaging, in a dry environment at a moderate temperature, and inspect them when the maintenance pool is reviewed. LED modules do not deteriorate meaningfully in normal storage conditions over the service life of the display, and a module rated for 100,000 hours of operation is not degraded by sitting unused. The practical risk is physical damage or moisture ingress in a poorly stored spare, so keeping the modules in their packaging with the desiccant in a dry location is the whole of the discipline.

Import Logistics and Market Entry

Modules ship inside the cabinets they belong to, which means the module specification and the mechanical interface arrive already matched to the screen rather than as separate parts to be assembled on site. For projects that include a spare parts allocation, the reserve modules are packed with the same shipment and identified on the packing list so that the on-site pool is established at commissioning rather than ordered reactively. Keeping the spares from the original production batch at the same time as the wall is the reason to include them in the initial order rather than adding them later.

Packing is designed around module fragility as well as cabinet weight. A fine-pitch module has a delicate surface and a coarse-pitch cabinet is heavy for its volume, so the two require different handling and the crate design reflects that. Panels are packed with the accessories belonging to the series, and the shipment documentation includes the conformity certificates needed for customs and for the local electrical inspection where the destination requires one.

Destination voltage and frequency are configured at the factory and labelled on the cabinet. Markets operating a mandatory certification scheme are routed through the appropriate approval process during production so that the consignment is not held at port. Freight is planned by crated volume and weight, with sea, air and rail options matched to the delivery window the site can accept, and phased projects split so that each phase arrives complete with its modules and accessories. Production is quoted at 15 to 20 working days.
