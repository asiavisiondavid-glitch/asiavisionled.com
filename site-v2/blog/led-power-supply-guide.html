The power supply is the least glamorous component in an LED display and the one most likely to decide whether the screen is still running in year five. A panel that looks flawless in a showroom can fail prematurely because the power supply was sized to the average load rather than the peak, or because the voltage arriving at the far cabinet of a large wall had already sagged below the driver's working range.

Getting the electrical design right is not complicated, but it does require the numbers to be settled before the order rather than after commissioning. This guide covers the power supply questions buyers ask most often, the configuration Asia Vision Technology recommends for different screen sizes, the power requirements of indoor and outdoor product families, and the support and logistics arrangements that follow an order.

FAQ: Power Supply Questions

Q: What output voltage do LED display power supplies use?
A: The overwhelming majority of indoor and outdoor LED modules are driven from a 5V DC supply, with some cabinet designs using 4.2V or 4.5V to reduce heat and improve driver efficiency. The power supply converts mains AC at 110V or 220V into that low-voltage DC rail, and the cabinet wiring then distributes it to module and driver boards. Because the rail is low voltage and the current is high, cable sizing on the DC side matters as much as the supply rating itself.
Q: How do I calculate the power a display actually needs?
A: Start from the panel's rated maximum and then apply a realistic duty factor. LED content is rarely all-white at full brightness, so the working load of typical video is well below the rated peak, but the supply should still be selected against the peak rather than the average so that a bright frame does not trip protection. A practical rule is to size the power supply above the calculated peak with a safety margin in the region of twenty to thirty per cent, which absorbs inrush current and leaves headroom for ageing.
Q: Should I specify a single large power supply or several smaller units?
A: For anything larger than a handful of cabinets, distributed supplies are the better engineering choice. Several medium-rated units spread across the wall share the thermal load, limit the impact of a single failure to one section of the screen, and shorten the DC cable runs that cause voltage drop. A single large supply concentrates heat in one place and makes any fault a whole-wall event, which is why multi-cabinet installations generally use one supply per cabinet or per small cabinet group.
Q: What is power factor correction and does it matter for an LED wall?
A: Power factor describes how efficiently the supply converts the current it draws into useful work, and a poor power factor means the installation pulls more current than the real load requires. Active power factor correction brings the figure close to unity, which matters on a large wall because it reduces the current the building has to deliver and can affect the rating of the protective device. Sites that meter apparent power rather than real power will also see the difference on the electricity bill.
Q: How much heat does a display power supply generate and how is it managed?
A: Supply losses appear as heat, and a supply running at higher efficiency wastes proportionally less of the load as thermal energy. Heat management in a cabinet is mostly about airflow and derating: supplies that are enclosed, dusty or stacked with no ventilation loose life quickly, while a sealed outdoor cabinet needs the supply derated for the internal temperature it will actually see. Mounting orientation and clearance around the unit matter as much as the headline efficiency figure.
Q: What causes a power supply to fail early?
A: The common causes are chronic overload on a supply sized to the average load, sustained high internal temperature from poor cabinet airflow or a dusty installation, mains transients where no surge protection is fitted, and loose DC terminations that create resistance and localised heating. Nearly all of these are specification or installation issues rather than manufacturing defects, which is why sizing, derating and torque-checking the terminations at commissioning are the three habits that most reliably extend service life.

What Power Supply Configuration Does Asia Vision Recommend?

The recommended configuration for any multi-cabinet screen is a distributed arrangement in which each cabinet, or a small group of cabinets, carries its own supply rather than drawing from one central unit. This spreads heat across the wall, limits the consequences of a failure to a defined section, and keeps the DC run short enough that the voltage drop between supply and module stays inside the driver's working range. It also makes the wall easier to service, because a supply swap happens inside one cabinet instead of at a single point the whole screen depends on.

Sizing follows the same discipline in every series. The supply is selected against the cabinet's peak rated load with a margin in the region of twenty to thirty per cent, and the resulting current is used to size the protective device and the input cabling. In practical terms, that means the electrical contractor receives a per-cabinet figure that can be multiplied by the cabinet count to give the total connected load, with diversity applied only where the site metering or the distribution design genuinely calls for it rather than as a shortcut to a smaller feed.

Redundancy is decided by the criticality of the screen rather than by wall size. A retail promotional panel can accept a single supply per cabinet and a planned maintenance response, while a control room, a transport information board or a live event wall is usually configured with a redundant supply path so that one failure does not darken the screen during operation. Signal and power should be routed so that the two paths do not share a single point of failure, since redundancy that relies on the same connector or breaker is not redundancy at all.

Finally, the configuration should account for the power quality at the site. Where the mains supply is unstable, surge protection at the cabinet input and, for exposed outdoor structures, a properly earthed lightning protection path are part of the recommendation rather than an accessory. Voltage that fluctuates beyond the supply's input range will shorten component life whatever the load calculation says, and a site survey that records the actual supply characteristics is a more reliable basis for the design than an assumption based on the building type.

What Are the Power Requirements for Different LED Types?

Power behaviour varies with pitch, brightness and environment, and those variables change the numbers enough that a single rule of thumb is unreliable. A fine-pitch indoor panel packs more drivers into the same area and generally draws a higher load per square metre at the same brightness than a coarse-pitch panel, while an outdoor cabinet driven at high brightness to compete with daylight draws more again. Sealed outdoor enclosures also run hotter internally, which forces the supply to be derated and therefore rated higher than the raw load would suggest.

FAQ: Additional Questions

Q: Can a display run on a generator supply at an outdoor event?
A: Yes, provided the generator is sized for the inrush current that the supplies draw at switch-on, which is higher than the steady running load. Voltage and frequency from a generator can also drift more than from a grid connection, so the supplies need to tolerate a wider input range or the site needs conditioning equipment. For touring and event work the practical approach is to size the generator against the peak rating of the wall plus the rest of the stage load, and to sequence power-up in sections rather than energising the entire wall at once.
Q: Do I need a separate distribution board for an LED wall?
A: For any substantial installation, a dedicated circuit or distribution board for the display is the cleaner design. It isolates the screen from other loads on the site, makes the protective device rating straightforward to match to the wall's peak load, and lets a fault on the display be isolated without interrupting unrelated equipment. On a large wall the board also gives a natural place for a main isolator, which is normally what an electrical inspector will expect to see during commissioning.
Q: What maintenance does the power section of a display need?
A: The routine work is inspection rather than repair: check the terminations for tightness, clear dust from the cabinet airflow path, and confirm that no supply is running noticeably hotter than its neighbours. Loose DC terminations create resistance and localised heating, so a torque check during annual maintenance prevents a failure that would otherwise appear without warning. Where a site is dusty or the cabinets are sealed and exposed, shortening the inspection interval is more effective than adding protection after the fact.
Q: How does brightness setting affect power consumption over the life of the screen?
A: Power draw scales broadly with brightness, so a wall run below its maximum output consumes less than the rated peak and generates less heat, both of which extend component life. This is one reason a dimming schedule is worth configuring even where it is not required by local lighting rules: running an indoor screen at a modest level after hours reduces thermal cycling on the supply. The saving is real but should not be used to justify an undersized supply, because the peak load case still governs the rating.
Q: What certifications apply to the power section of an LED display?
A: The complete product is certified as a unit rather than the supply in isolation, and Asia Vision products hold CE, FCC and RoHS certification, with UL and ETL paths available for North American installations and UKCA documentation for the United Kingdom. For the electrical inspector, the document that usually matters is the conformity certificate for the finished product together with the cabinet input rating and the load schedule, all of which are supplied with the shipment so that commissioning is not delayed by a missing document.
Q: Can a small display be powered from a standard wall outlet?
A: A small screen of a few cabinets can normally run from a standard circuit, but the circuit rating and the supply's input current should still be checked rather than assumed, particularly on a 110V supply where the same load draws roughly twice the current of a 220V installation. Where the site has limited circuits available, distributing the wall across two circuits is a practical way to stay within the rating of each, and it also gives a degree of separation if one circuit trips during an event.

Import Logistics and Market Entry

LED cabinets ship with the power supply already fitted and configured for the destination market, which is the arrangement that avoids the most common import complaint: a screen that arrives correctly built but unusable because the local mains standard differs from the factory default. Asia Vision builds to the destination voltage and frequency and labels the input rating on the cabinet, so a 110V North American installation and a 220V European or Middle Eastern site are both supplied ready to energise. Certification documentation accompanies the shipment so that customs and the electrical inspector see the same evidence.

Shipping is planned by volume and weight rather than by cabinet count, because a fine-pitch wall and a coarse-pitch facade can differ substantially in crated density for a similar screen area. Cabinets are crated with the power and signal accessories packed alongside so that installation does not stall waiting for a separate consignment, and larger projects are commonly split into phased shipments so that the first phase can be installed while the remainder is in transit. Production is quoted at 15 to 20 working days, and sea freight, air freight and rail options are matched to the delivery date the site can actually accept.

Customs treatment depends on the destination, and the practical preparation is documentation rather than guesswork. Commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin and the relevant conformity certificates are prepared per shipment, and where a market requires a local registration or a pre-shipment inspection, that step is scheduled into the production timeline rather than treated as an afterthought. Countries with a mandatory certification scheme are handled through the appropriate route so that the consignment is not held at port awaiting a document that could have been raised at the factory.

Equipment Comparison

Comparing power designs across suppliers is difficult because most publish a watts-per-square-metre figure that hides the variables that actually determine reliability. The more useful comparison is the per-cabinet rating, the efficiency of the supply, whether power factor correction is included, and whether the suggested supply has headroom over the cabinet's peak load. A lower headline consumption figure achieved by under-sizing the supply is not an efficiency gain; it is a service life reduction deferred to a date after the warranty period. A design that meets the load figure at the first cabinet but allows the DC voltage to sag below the driver range at the last one will produce a screen with a visible brightness gradient that increases as the wall ages. Distributing supplies and checking the voltage drop across the full run is the practical way to compare two suppliers whose headline figures look identical.

Technical Support and Warranty

Power faults are diagnosable, and the fastest route back to a working screen is a supplier who can interpret symptoms rather than a supplier who ships a replacement and hopes. Asia Vision Technology supports installations through documented wiring and load information, cabinet-level fault isolation guidance, and factory contact for cases where a symptom points to a driver or supply interaction rather than to a single failed unit. Because the supply is fitted per cabinet, a fault on one section is normally resolved by a swap that does not require the whole wall to come down.

Panels carry a three-year warranty and a rated 100,000 hour service life, and the warranty covers the fitted power supply alongside the module and driver components rather than treating the electrical section as a consumable. That matters because in practice a disproportionate share of early LED failures trace back to power rather than to emitters, and a warranty that excluded the supply would leave the most probable failure mode outside cover.

For installations that cannot tolerate downtime, the recommended arrangement is a small pool of spare supplies and modules held on site or at a nearby service point, sized to the number of cabinets and the maintenance round. Spare supplies are interchangeable within a series, so a single spare unit can cover a section of wall rather than a specific cabinet. Asia Vision also supplies replacement units after the warranty period, with production quoted at 15 to 20 working days for orders placed against an operating screen.
