An LED display warranty is a written promise that the supplier will repair or replace any part that fails because of a defect in materials or workmanship, and it is defined by three things: what is covered, how long it lasts, and the date the clock starts. In practice, most covered failures are electrical — a module, a driver IC, or a power supply that dies early — while most exclusions are environmental or human: water ingress, lightning and surge damage, misuse, and cosmetic wear. Read those two lists before you sign, because they decide how much of the screen's life you carry and how much your supplier carries.

What an LED Display Warranty Actually Covers

A standard LED display warranty covers defects in materials and workmanship for a fixed term. That means the parts that fail on their own: LED modules, driver ICs, receiving cards, power supplies, and the cabinet and connectors. It does not cover damage caused by the site, the weather, or the operator, however the failure looks on the screen.

The distinction that matters is cause, not symptom. A dark module could be a defective driver IC, which is covered, or a flooded cabinet, which is not. Two screens can show the same fault and receive opposite answers, because the warranty turns on why the part failed. That is why a claim needs evidence, and why the exclusions list deserves more attention than the coverage list. Coverage tells you what is promised; exclusions tell you what you must prevent yourself.

For the display types we build, from indoor fine-pitch COB panels to outdoor S-series cabinets, the covered components are the same set: the light engines, the drive electronics, the power stage, and the mechanical frame. The excluded causes are also the same, and they are the ones an installation can control.

The Four Failure Types a Warranty Is Built to Catch

An LED display is thousands of components on a shared power and signal path, and the faults a warranty is designed for fall into four groups: light-output failures, driver and control failures, power-stage failures, and mechanical or structural failures. Each wears differently and leaves different evidence, so each is argued about differently when a claim is filed.

Light-output failures are the visible ones: a single dead pixel, a cluster of dark LEDs, a module that reads a colour wrong, or a line of dead pixels. They are usually the easiest to prove, because a photograph and a pixel count settle the question. Driver and control failures show as flicker, ghosting, a wrong colour across a whole module, or a card that drops its section of the screen. Power-stage failures cut a module or a cabinet, and they often follow a brownout or a bad connector. Mechanical failures are structural — a bent frame, a failed connector, a cabinet that no longer seals — and they are the ones most often disputed, because impact and water both leave similar marks.

A well-written warranty names each group and states the evidence required. When the failure type is not named, the supplier and the owner argue about category rather than cause, and the claim stalls. The table below sorts the four groups by cause, cover, and proof.

What Every LED Warranty Excludes — Water, Lightning, Misuse, and Wear

Every warranty has an exclusion list, and for LED displays the exclusions cluster around four causes: water, electrical surge, misuse, and normal wear. These are excluded because the environment, the installation, or the operator caused them rather than the product, and each exclusion tells you which protection you must add yourself.

Water ingress is the most common exclusion and the most expensive to get wrong. It appears when a cabinet is not sealed to the specification, when a gasket is reused after service, or when drainage is blocked. Lightning and surge damage is next: a strike on the building's wiring can reach the display through the power or data path and destroy a power supply, a receiving card, or a row of drivers, none of which a manufacturer warranty covers. Misuse covers operation outside the rated conditions — running a display past its rated brightness or temperature, hot-swapping cards, or tampering with the configuration — and unauthorised repair voids cover on the part that was touched. Wear items such as fans, filters, and seals are expected to need replacement and are normally excluded.

The practical lesson is that these exclusions do not leave you unprotected; they tell you what to add. Fit surge protection and proper earthing for the electrical risk. Seal and commission the cabinet correctly for the water risk. Train operators so they stay inside the rated conditions. Each of these measures protects the part the warranty excludes.

  • Physical damage from handling, impact, or vibration in transit
  • Water ingress caused by the installation or a failed seal
  • Damage from lightning, surges, or an unstable supply
  • Unauthorised repair, modification, or hot-swapping
  • Wear items such as fans, filters, and seals
  • Operation outside the rated temperature, brightness, or voltage

When Does the Warranty Clock Actually Start?

The warranty clock can start on one of three dates — the ship date, the arrival date, or the acceptance date — and the gap between them can be months. On a long ocean shipment or a staged installation, cover counted from shipment can quietly expire while the screen is still in its crate, so the start date matters as much as the term.

Ship-date warranties are the supplier's default and the buyer's worst case, because every day in transit or in storage is a day of cover lost. Arrival-date warranties move the start to delivery at the port or site, which recovers the transit time but still loses any period before commissioning. Acceptance-date or commissioning-date warranties start when the screen is tested and handed over, which gives the owner the full term in service. For a project with a long lead time or a staged handover, the difference between these dates can be the difference between two years of cover and eighteen months.

Writing the Term and Start Date Into the Purchase Order

A warranty is only as strong as the document that defines it, so the term and the start date belong in the purchase order, not in a brochure. State the length, the triggering event, and the coverage in the PO, and require the supplier to confirm them in writing before you place the order.

The purchase order should carry the whole warranty in one place: the term, the start event, the covered components, the exclusions, the dead-pixel threshold, who pays freight on a claim, the spare-parts availability window, and the claim procedure with named contacts. A single paragraph of terms is worth more than several pages of marketing. If the supplier resists putting any of these in writing, treat that as information about how a future claim will go.

  • Term length and the exact start event
  • Covered components and named exclusions
  • Dead-pixel or defect threshold, in numbers
  • Freight responsibility for warranty parts, both ways
  • Spare-parts availability for the model
  • Claim procedure, response expectation, and named contacts

How an LED Warranty Claim Runs From Report to Repair

A claim runs through a repeatable sequence: you report the fault with evidence, the supplier evaluates it, both sides agree on the cause, and the fix is shipped or scheduled. A good claim is documented from the first photograph, because the supplier's decision rests on cause, and cause is proved with records rather than described in words.

The report is the part you control. Photograph the fault, note the date and the module position, record the serial numbers, and attach the purchase order and the installation record, then send it to the named claim contact rather than a general address. The supplier then tests or inspects, decides whether the failure is a covered defect or an excluded cause, and issues a replacement or a repair instruction. Where a spare is held on site you restore the screen at once and return the failed part afterwards; where it is not, you wait for the part to arrive. The table sets out the usual division of effort.

DecisionConfirm the agreed cause and scopeApprove, replace, or repair under the term

Who Pays Freight When a Warranty Part Fails

Freight is the hidden cost in almost every warranty dispute, so the contract should say who pays it in each direction. A warranty that covers the part but not the shipping turns a covered fault into a bill, and on a large cabinet or module the freight can approach the value of the replacement itself.

The usual arrangements run from owner-pays-both-ways, which is the weakest term, to supplier-pays-both-ways, which is the strongest. Between them sit the common compromises: the supplier ships the replacement free and the owner returns the failed part at its own cost, or each side pays its own direction. For a small part such as a receiving card or a driver board, the freight is minor and the arrangement matters little. For a full cabinet or a power supply in an outdoor product it can dominate the claim, so write it down. Where batch-matched spares are held on site, freight falls away almost entirely, because only the failed part travels back.

Replace or Repair? What Decides the Outcome

Most warranties let the supplier choose between replacing a failed part and repairing it, and the choice turns on cost, age, and availability. A failed receiving card is normally swapped; a cabinet fault is often repaired in place; a module is usually replaced whole, because field repair of a sealed module is neither reliable nor quick.

The distinction matters to downtime and to the warranty's future. A replaced part usually carries either its own fresh term or the balance of the original, so ask which applies. A repaired part carries the original clock. If the model is near the end of its production life, a replacement may come as a compatible part rather than an identical one, which is acceptable only if it matches the pitch, brightness, and batch colour of the rest of the wall. Write the acceptance rule for a compatible replacement into the terms, so a near-match does not show as a patch on the screen.

Keeping the Screen Running While a Claim Is Open

Downtime, not the part cost, is what hurts most owners, and the fix is to hold batch-matched spares on site so a failed module can be swapped in minutes while the claim is settled. The warranty replaces the part; the spare keeps the screen on air while the paperwork runs.

A spare-parts programme works with the warranty rather than against it. You fit a matching spare, restore the display, and return the failed part for replacement under the term, so the screen is never dark waiting for a shipment. For a business-critical screen — a control room, a broadcast wall, or a trading floor — the spare is the real insurance, not the warranty, because the warranty pays for the part and the spare protects the uptime. Our spare-parts inventory guide sets out how many units to hold and how to keep them batch-matched.

Where a loaner part is offered as part of an extended cover, ask what is loaned and how fast it arrives, because a loaner that reaches you after the event has not protected the uptime. The relevant question is always continuity, not only the repair cost.

The Dead-Pixel Threshold: How Many Failed Pixels Are Acceptable

A warranty does not replace a module for a single dead pixel; it replaces it above a written threshold, and that threshold decides whether a screen with a scattering of failed LEDs is covered. The number must be in the contract, because a vague threshold turns every claim into a negotiation.

Thresholds are expressed in different ways, and the language matters. Some contracts set a percentage of total pixels; others set a count per module or per square metre; others use a rule about the distance between failed pixels, so that isolated dots are tolerated while clusters are not. A screen can look acceptable at a distance with a low density of dead pixels yet fail a customer's expectations, so the threshold should match the use. For a close-viewed indoor wall the practical tolerance is tighter than for an outdoor billboard read from the road.

Two points protect you. First, get the threshold in writing and confirm how it is measured — per module, per cabinet, or for the whole wall. Second, agree how a failed pixel is counted: a fully dead pixel and a stuck or off-colour pixel may be treated differently, so the definition belongs in the terms too. Bear in mind that a slow, even loss of output across the whole wall is ageing rather than failure, as our LED display lifespan guide explains; the table below shows the common ways a defect limit is expressed and what each means for a claim.

Do Colour Shift and Brightness Degradation Count as Warranty Faults?

Brightness decay and colour shift are usually treated as normal ageing rather than defects, so a gradual dimming over years is not a warranty claim; a sudden or abnormal shift within the term can be. The dividing line is rate and pattern, not the fact of change, and it should be defined before the screen ships.

Every LED ages: output falls slowly and colour drifts a little, which is why manufacturers bin LEDs by brightness and wavelength and why a wall is built from matched batches. A slow, even decay across the whole wall is expected and excluded as wear. A fault happens when one module or cabinet drops faster than its neighbours and reads as a patch of a different colour or brightness, or when a wall fails to hold calibration within a stated window. That is a defect, and it belongs in the warranty.

The protection for a batch-colour fault is the spare, not only the warranty, because the replacement must match the batch that is already ageing on the wall. When a model is discontinued, matching becomes harder, which is another reason to hold batch-matched spares and to ask about the parts-availability window at the buying stage. Is an Extended LED Warranty Worth Buying?

An extended warranty is worth buying when the cost of a fault across the extended years exceeds the fee, which is true for critical, hard-worked, or hard-to-service screens and rarely true for lightly used ones. It is insurance on the screen's later years, so the decision is the same as any insurance decision: weigh the premium against the loss.

The strongest case is a screen whose downtime is expensive — a control room, a broadcast wall, a transport hub — or one that works in a harsh environment, at high brightness, or for long daily hours, where components age faster. The weaker case is a low-use display in a clean, cool indoor space, where the parts that fail early tend to fail under the standard cover and the rest last well beyond the extended years. Match the decision to the duty cycle, not to a feature count, and compare the fee with the realistic repair cost over the same period. How Extended-Warranty Pricing Is Structured

An extended-warranty fee is built from scope, term, and risk, not from the screen's price alone. Scope decides what is included, term decides how much risk the supplier carries, and the screen's duty cycle and environment set how likely a claim is, so two identical screens in different sites can price differently.

Ask what the fee buys across five dimensions. Scope: parts only, parts and labour, or parts, labour, and travel. Term: the added years and the exact start. Response: the contractual time to react, which is separate from the repair time. Continuity: whether spares and loaner parts are guaranteed for the added years. Conditions: whether scheduled maintenance is required to keep the cover valid. A fee quoted for parts only, with the owner carrying labour and travel, is not comparable to a comprehensive fee, so compare like with like. We publish no fixed extended-warranty prices, because the term, scope, duty cycle, and environment differ by project; ask your account contact for a structure that matches your site rather than a headline number.

The maintenance condition deserves a flag. Many extended warranties stay valid only if the screen is serviced on a schedule, which may be reasonable, since a maintained screen fails less, but it adds a cost that should be counted with the fee. A maintenance contract and an extended warranty are different products: the contract prevents faults by servicing the screen, while the warranty covers the faults that still occur. For a critical installation the two work best together, as our maintenance contract guide explains.

Matching the Warranty to Spares, Maintenance, and Screen Life

A warranty is one of three supports for an LED display, and it protects the screen's life best when paired with spares and maintenance rather than relied on alone. The warranty covers defects; the spare protects uptime; maintenance reduces faults — and the three together decide the cost of the whole life.

Plan the end of the term before it arrives. Decide whether to extend the cover, move to a maintenance contract, or rely on batch-matched spares and in-house service, and make that decision with the screen's duty cycle in front of you. A screen that reaches the end of its warranty with no plan carries every future fault alone, which is a cost forward planning avoids. Where you source matters too: a direct manufacturer can confirm parts continuity and let you inspect the build, as a visit to our factory shows, and our range is set out in the product catalogue. Keep the warranty, the PO, the inspection report, and the serial numbers together, and your entire term runs cleaner.

Warranty Expectations in North America

US warranty expectation is shaped by the fact that the buyer is often a reseller with an obligation to an end user, so the warranty term is passed on rather than consumed.

Three features matter. The term and what it covers, stated in terms a reseller can pass down without rewriting. The remedy — repair, replace or credit — and who pays the freight, since on a US project the freight can exceed the part. And the evidence required to make a claim, because a claim that depends on a description alone will be disputed by whoever carries it up the chain.

Two practices reduce disputes. Record the module batch and firmware version with each installation, so a replacement matches the wall. And keep the fault record in a form that can be shown to an owner who did not see the failure — a date-stamped description and a photograph taken before the module is removed.

FAQ
