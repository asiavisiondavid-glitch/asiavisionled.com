What does an Algeria LED display importer need to know? Importing LED displays into Algeria means clearing a regulated product through a conformity assessment programme, a bank domiciliation file, and customs at Algiers or Oran. This 2026 guide explains the IANOR standards, the Certificate of Conformity, duties, VAT, and the questions to ask a supplier.

Algeria is the largest country in Africa by area and one of the more tightly regulated import markets in North Africa. LED displays are required for retail chains, mosques, stadiums, transport hubs, banks, and public sector projects, and demand has grown as the country invests in modern commercial infrastructure.

An Algeria LED display importer who completes the conformity file and the bank formalities before the cargo sails clears it without difficulty. The steps that catch buyers out are the pre-shipment certificate and the currency domiciliation, both of which must be arranged before the goods leave the factory.

Customs Entry for an Algeria LED Display Importer

Import clearance is handled by the Direction Generale des Douanes, known as the DGD. The importer needs a tax identification number, a customs importer code, and a registered commercial address before the first entry can be lodged.

Algeria also requires bank domiciliation for imports. The invoice is registered with an approved bank before shipment, and payment is normally made through a documentary credit or a transfer approved by the bank. Without a domiciliation file the customs entry cannot proceed.

Ledger the timing carefully. The domiciliation must be in place before the goods ship, and the conformity certificate must be issued against the same shipment. A cargo that arrives without both documents is held at the port while the file is rebuilt, and demurrage accumulates daily.

IANOR Standards and Conformity Assessment

The national standards body is the Institut Algerien de Normalisation, IANOR, which publishes the Algerian standards and coordinates conformity assessment for regulated products. Algeria operates a conformity programme under which regulated imports must be accompanied by a Certificate of Conformity, usually called a COC.

The certificate is issued by an accredited inspection body after a documentary review and, where required, a physical inspection at the loading point. The supplier submits the test reports, the invoice, and the packing list, and the body verifies that the goods match the declared specifications.

Confirm that the certificate names the exact model and the exact quantity being shipped. A COC covering a different model in the same range will not clear the consignment, and the correction must be made before the goods are loaded, not after they arrive.

Duty, VAT, and the Landed Cost

Algeria applies a customs duty on imported goods at a rate that depends on the tariff line, and value added tax at the standard rate on the duty-paid value. Some categories also attract an internal consumption tax. The freight and insurance are included in the customs value.

Build the landed cost from the customs value rather than from the factory price. Duty is charged on the goods plus freight plus insurance, and the tax is then charged on that total, so freight has a multiplied effect on the final figure.

Working With an Algeria Customs Broker

Clearance in Algeria is normally handled by a licensed customs broker, a commissionnaire en douane agree. The broker lodges the entry, presents the certificate and the domiciliation file, and manages the inspection at the port.

Choose a broker who works regularly at the port you are using. Algiers, Oran, Annaba, Bejaia, and Skikda each have their own procedural habits and their own inspection queues, and a broker who knows the terminal saves days on the release.

Agree the classification of the goods in writing before the first shipment. A change of tariff line after the goods are loaded forces an amendment, and an amendment at the port is slower and more expensive than a decision made at the desk.

Specifying for Algerian Conditions

Algeria has a Mediterranean climate along the northern coast and a desert climate inland and in the south. Screens near the coast face humid summers and salt air, while inland and southern sites face extreme heat, dust, and very large day to night temperature swings.

For Algerian sites, humidity and salt-laden coastal air make sealed cabinets the deciding factor: the COB EF outdoor series (IP65, 3000 nits) and the COB ZH touring series (sealed, 3400–4000 nits) are built for exactly this duty.

Power supply quality varies across the country, so specify drivers and power supplies that tolerate a wide input range and consider a voltage stabiliser at the cabinet level. A screen that is specified only for a clean grid will fail early on a fluctuating one.

For an outdoor site, match the brightness to the actual ambient light level and the viewing distance, and provide front or rear service access according to the mounting structure. In desert conditions, service access is the design decision that most affects the lifetime cost of ownership.

Ask every candidate the same questions and record the answers in writing. For an Algerian project the conformity evidence, the power supply specification, and the spare parts commitment matter as much as the unit price.

Confirm the components in writing and require written approval for any substitution. A supplier facing a component shortage may change a driver or a power supply quietly, and the change appears only when the screens are unpacked on site.

Common Mistakes Algeria Importers Make

  • Shipping before the bank domiciliation is approved
  • Loading cargo without a Certificate of Conformity for the exact model
  • Declaring a mixed shipment under a single tariff code
  • Forgetting to include freight and insurance in the customs value
  • Ordering a screen specified for a temperate climate for a desert site
  • Leaving the spare parts commitment out of the purchase contract
  • Choosing a broker who does not work at the arrival port

Each of these mistakes is inexpensive to avoid during planning and expensive after the goods arrive. An Algeria LED display importer who completes the conformity file and the bank formalities before ordering, and who specifies for heat and dust, avoids most of them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What does an Algeria LED display importer need before the first shipment?
A: Register with the tax authority, obtain a customs importer code, complete the bank domiciliation for the invoice, and arrange a Certificate of Conformity for the exact model being shipped. All of these must be in place before the goods leave the factory.
Q: What is the Certificate of Conformity for Algeria?
A: It is a pre-shipment certificate issued by an accredited inspection body confirming that the consignment meets the applicable Algerian standards. Customs clearance requires it, and it must name the exact model and quantity shipped.
Q: Which standards body publishes the Algerian requirements?
A: The national standards body is the Institut Algerien de Normalisation, IANOR. It publishes the Algerian standards and coordinates the conformity assessment programme that regulated imports must satisfy.
Q: Which ports handle LED display imports into Algeria?
A: The main commercial ports are Algiers and Oran, with Annaba, Bejaia, and Skikda also handling container traffic. Choose a customs broker who works regularly at your arrival port.
Q: What specification suits Algerian desert sites?
A: Specify high brightness, a stated operating temperature range above the site maximum, filtered cooling for dust, corrosion-resistant hardware at coastal sites, and a wide-range power supply to tolerate grid fluctuation.
