By David

A dvLED wall is a drawn element before it is a product: it has dimensions, a depth, a weight, a service requirement and a surface finish, and all of those belong in the design rather than in a coordination note added later. Designers who treat it as equipment to be scheduled tend to rediscover it during construction, when the options have narrowed.

This guide covers what a design team needs to establish about a dvLED wall, and where it interacts with the rest of the building.

What the Wall Actually Is, Physically

A dvLED wall is an assembly of LED cabinets mounted on a structure. Each cabinet carries modules that produce the image, so the visible surface is a tiled plane rather than a single panel.

Three physical facts drive the design. The wall has depth: cabinets plus structure plus a service allowance behind or beside it. It has weight, concentrated on the mounting points rather than spread evenly. And it has a service requirement, meaning a route to every module, ideally from the face so no void is needed behind.

Those three facts belong in the drawing at the same stage as the finishes, not after them.

Sightlines and the Geometry of the Room

The image has to work from where people actually are, and that is a design question rather than a technical one.

Three measurements decide it. The nearest position from which the wall will be viewed, which sets the finest pitch needed. The farthest, which sets the coarsest that remains usable. And the off-axis positions, because a wall read at an angle loses brightness and colour compared with one read head-on.

Where the room curves or the seating wraps, the off-axis view matters more than the head-on one, and it is the measurement most often missing from a brief.

Structure, Substrate and Fixing

The wall is fixed to something, and the substrate determines how.

On a masonry wall the fixing is direct and the question is capacity. On a steel frame the frame is designed for the load. On a stud partition or a dry-lined wall the display frequently needs its own support, because the partition was not designed to carry it.

Two points are worth settling early. Whether the display is flush with the finish or proud of it, since that decides the depth of the reveal and the shadow line. And whether the wall is to be recessed, because a recess has to be sized for the cabinet plus the mounting plus the airflow, and adjusting a recess later is a builder’s change.

Finishes, Reveals and Coordination

The junction between the screen and the surrounding material is where a display reads as designed or as installed.

Three details recur. The reveal around the screen, which can be a shadow gap, a trim or a flush joint, and which sets the tolerance the installation has to work to. The return to the adjacent finish, because the cabinet depth has to be absorbed somewhere. And the location of the power and signal, which should be coordinated with the ceiling or floor strategy rather than discovered as a surface run.

Where the wall follows a curve, the same cabinets can be set at an angle to each other, which is how a curved surface is built without a bespoke panel. That option is worth knowing at the design stage, because it changes what shapes are achievable.

What to Ask For at Design Stage

The information that makes the design work comes from the manufacturer, and it should be requested before the details are fixed.

The cabinet dimensions and weight, so the support and the reveal can be designed. The depth including the mounting and the service allowance. The power and data requirements, so the services can be routed. And the finish options for the visible edge and the surrounding trim.

A manufacturer who can supply those at design stage makes the coordination straightforward; one who supplies them at order stage moves the risk into construction.

FAQ

Q: Can a dvLED wall follow a curve?
A: Yes. Curved surfaces are built by setting standard cabinets at small angles to each other rather than by using bespoke panels, which means a curve can be designed without a special order.
Q: How deep does a dvLED wall need to be?
A: Depth is the cabinet plus the mounting plus whatever service allowance is needed. Where modules are serviceable from the front, no void behind the wall is required, which usually removes a significant part of the depth.
Q: Does the wall need its own support?
A: It depends on the substrate. A masonry or steel structure can often carry the wall directly, while a stud or dry-lined partition frequently cannot and needs independent support behind the finish.
Q: What viewing distance should be designed for?
A: Both the nearest and the farthest position matter, and so do the off-axis views. The nearest position sets the pitch; the off-axis positions determine whether the image holds up where the room curves or the seating wraps.
