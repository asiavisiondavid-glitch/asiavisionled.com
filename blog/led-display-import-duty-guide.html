What is led display import duty? Led display import duty is the tax a country charges when LED screens cross its border, calculated as a percentage of the goods value and set by the product's classification code. Duty changes the landed cost, so buyers must estimate it before comparing quotes. This 2026 guide explains HS codes, duty rates, and how to reduce what you pay.

Two quotes that look identical can land at very different costs once duty is added. A buyer who ignores duty compares only the factory price, and the first shipment surprises them at the border. Understanding duty turns that surprise into a planned cost, and sometimes into a saving.

This guide is written for importers of LED displays. It explains how duty is calculated, how the product is classified, and the legitimate ways to reduce the duty bill. It is general guidance; the exact rate for your country should be confirmed with a customs broker.

How Import Duty Is Calculated

Import duty is usually a percentage of the customs value of the goods. The customs value is normally the price paid for the goods, plus certain costs such as transport and insurance to the port of entry, depending on the country's rules. Duty is added before the import tax is calculated, so the two stack.

Customs valueGoods price plus adjustmentsCountry rules vary
Duty ratePercentage by HS codeSet by the destination
Duty amountCustoms value times rateAdded before tax

Landed costAll of the aboveWhat you actually pay

Import tax, often a VAT or GST, is calculated on the value plus the duty. This is why duty matters more than its own percentage suggests: it raises the base for the tax as well. A duty rate of a few percent can add more to the final cost than buyers expect.

HS Codes for LED Displays

Every product carries a Harmonised System code, an international classification that decides the duty rate. LED displays usually fall under a heading for electronic display or lighting equipment, but the exact code depends on the product and the country. The wrong code means the wrong rate and a possible penalty.

Confirm the HS code with a customs broker before the first shipment. The same screen can be classified differently depending on how it is described and used, and the classification affects the duty. A broker who knows LED products will classify correctly and avoid a costly error.

Duty planning checklist:
✅ Confirm the HS code with a customs broker
✅ Check the duty rate for your country
✅ Include freight and insurance in the customs value
✅ Remember the import tax stacks on duty
✅ Ask for a certificate of origin where relevant
✅ Budget the full landed cost before ordering

Landed Cost and Quote Comparison

Landed cost is the true cost of an import: goods, freight, insurance, duty, tax, and any handling fees. Two suppliers with the same factory price can have different landed costs if their shipping terms differ. Comparing landed cost, not factory price, is the only fair comparison.

An accurate landed cost model needs real cabinet weight and packaging dimensions; both are listed per series in our LED display series overview.

Build a simple landed-cost sheet for every quote. List the goods value, the freight, the insurance, the duty, and the tax, and add them. The supplier with the lowest landed cost wins, which is sometimes not the one with the lowest factory price. This sheet also shows the real impact of an Incoterm choice.

Reducing Duty Legitimately

There are legal ways to reduce duty, and illegal ones that carry heavy penalties. The legal routes include correct classification, using a trade agreement's preferential rate, and declaring the value accurately. Under-declaring the value to pay less duty is fraud, and customs authorities investigate it.

  • Classify the product correctly to avoid overpaying
  • Use a certificate of origin for a trade agreement rate
  • Declare the value accurately, never under-declare
  • Consider component import where it lowers the rate
  • Keep records to support every declared value

Some countries charge a lower rate on components than on finished products, which can make importing parts and assembling locally cheaper. This should be planned with a broker, because the rules are specific and the savings depend on the product and the market. It is a legitimate option worth checking for large projects.

Certificates of Origin

A certificate of origin states where the goods were made, and it can unlock a reduced duty rate under a trade agreement. Many countries have agreements that lower or remove duty for goods from certain origins. Without the certificate, the goods pay the standard rate even if they qualify.

Ask your supplier for the origin documents with the shipment. A certificate obtained after the goods arrive is harder to apply, and the duty may already have been paid at the standard rate. Getting the paperwork right at shipment is the simplest way to secure the reduced rate.

De Minimis and Small Shipments

Many countries have a de minimis threshold below which no duty is charged. Small shipments, such as samples or a few spare modules, may clear below this threshold and arrive duty-free. For larger orders, the threshold is exceeded and duty applies to the whole value.

The threshold differs by country and can change. Check it before assuming a small shipment is duty-free, and confirm how the value is calculated. A shipment split across several parcels does not usually avoid duty if customs considers it a single commercial order.

Working with a Customs Broker

A customs broker handles the classification, the entry, and the duty payment on your behalf. For a first import, a broker is close to essential, because the paperwork and the codes are easy to get wrong. A good broker also advises on the cheapest legal route to import.

Choose a broker who knows electronics and LED products. A generalist broker may misclassify the goods or miss a preferential rate. Ask about their experience with displays and their fees, and use the same broker consistently so they learn your products over time.

Duty on a US Import

A US import duty is calculated from three inputs: the classification, the declared value, and the rate that applies to that classification and origin. Buyers who plan from a single percentage carried over from an earlier project are the ones who find the difference at entry.

Two things make the US position worth confirming per order rather than once. First, additional measures can apply on top of the general rate to goods of particular origin, and their scope and level change over time. Second, the declared value rules are specific, and a value that does not reflect the transaction can be corrected later at cost.

Three practices keep the exposure predictable. Confirm the classification with the broker before the order is placed. Confirm what, if any, additional measure applies to that classification and that origin at that date. And record the basis of the valuation in the entry file, so the figure can be explained if it is reviewed.

Where the duty changes the economics of a project, the usual responses are timing, a change in the port of entry, or a change in the specification — not a different declared value.

Planning Duty Into the Project

Build duty into the project budget from the start. A screen that looks affordable at the factory price may be expensive once duty and tax are added. Presenting the landed cost to the end client avoids the awkward moment when the invoice is higher than the quote.

When duty is budgeted from the start, cabinet configuration matters — the COB EF outdoor series and X Series list cabinet weight and packing data per model.

Keep a record of every shipment's classification, value, and duty paid. These records support future shipments and help if customs raises a question. Accurate, consistent documentation is the buyer's best protection in any duty matter.

Valuation Rules

The declared value drives the duty, so how it is calculated matters. Most countries use the transaction value, the price actually paid, adjusted for certain costs. The rules on what is included, such as freight and insurance, differ, so confirm the basis for your market with a broker.

Declare the value honestly and keep the invoice to support it. Customs authorities compare declared values against market data, and a value far below the norm invites a query, a reassessment, and sometimes a penalty. Saving a little duty by under-declaring risks the whole shipment and the supplier relationship.

Duty and Import Tax Together

Duty and import tax are separate charges, but they interact. The tax is calculated on the value plus the duty, so a duty increase raises the tax base as well. A buyer who estimates only the duty and forgets the tax will underestimate the landed cost, sometimes by a large margin.

Work the calculation through for a sample order before the first shipment. Take the goods value, add the freight and insurance to the point the country requires, apply the duty rate, then apply the tax rate to the total. The result is the true landed cost, and it is the number to compare between suppliers.

Led display import duty is a predictable cost once you understand the code, the rate, and the stacking with tax. Buyers who classify correctly, use origin documents, and compare landed cost avoid surprises and sometimes pay far less than the standard rate would suggest.
