A restaurant sells the room as much as the food, and screens now sit in almost every part of it: the drive-thru lane, the counter menu board, the pickup shelf, the dining-room wall. The most common mistake in hospitality projects is treating those positions as one problem and specifying a single panel type for all of them. Each position has its own reading distance, its own light environment, and its own tolerance for heat, grease and cleaning. This guide works through what actually decides the outcome zone by zone, so the specification you take to a supplier is built around the job the screen has to do rather than around a catalogue page.

Start With a Zone Map, Not a Product List

Before anyone discusses pixel pitch or output, list every position where a screen might go and record three things for each one: the furthest distance a person reads it from, whether the viewer can be in daylight or looking through glass, and what the air around the screen contains. A drive-thru confirmation screen read from a car at three or four metres is a different product from a pickup shelf read at arm's length, even though both are loosely called menu boards. Sorting the site this way takes an hour and prevents the classic error of buying one specification and forcing it into positions it was never built for.

The positions in a typical food and beverage venue fall into a handful of families, and grouping them early keeps every later decision honest.

  • Exterior and window signage: street-facing promotion read from the pavement or a passing car, and the most light-stressed position on the site.
  • Drive-thru approach, ordering and confirmation: outdoors and weather-exposed, read from inside a vehicle in daylight and after dark.
  • Counter menu boards: indoors but often in front of a glazed facade, read from two to six metres by customers deciding under queue pressure.
  • Order-confirmation and pickup screens: small and close-viewed at one to two metres, and the most likely to be bumped or splashed.
  • Kitchen order displays: seen by staff rather than customers, and living in the harshest air in the building.
  • Dining-room feature walls and dividers: atmosphere and sport, watched by seated guests across the room, where glare and noise matter more than raw output.

Pixel Pitch Follows the Furthest Reader, Not the Nearest

Pixel pitch is the spacing between LED clusters, and it sets the smallest detail an eye can resolve. The instinct is to specify the finest pitch the budget allows, but pitch should follow the furthest distance a viewer must read from, not the closest. A board watched from across a dining room can be coarser than one at the counter, because at distance the eye blends the pixels and the extra density is paid for and never seen. Spending on pitch is only worthwhile where the audience actually stands close enough to benefit.

Two related variables matter as much as the pitch figure. Font size has to be large relative to the pitch, because customers deciding under queue pressure read in a glance rather than a scan, and small text on a dense panel is still unreadable if nobody can see it. The arrangement of a multi-panel menu wall also has to keep its seams tight, since a visible gap running through the middle of a menu reads to the customer as a broken screen. Set the pitch from measured distances and the smallest text in your content, then confirm it against the table below rather than against a supplier's default.

Brightness Has to Beat the Window, Not the Room

Brightness decisions go wrong in both directions. A counter behind a large glazed front, or a board facing a west window, has to hold up against daylight, while a dim dining room is ruined by a panel specified for outdoors: the screen glares, flattens the food photography and washes the whole room. The working rule is to match output to the brightest ambient light the screen will ever face, then let it dim automatically as that light falls away through the day.

Automatic brightness control is worth specifying rather than leaving to a member of staff with a remote. A screen facing a window is brightest at midday and dimmest after dark, and a light sensor or a scheduled dimming profile follows that change without anyone touching it during service. It also cuts energy use, because the panel is never run at full output in a dark room. Glare, Colour and Making Food Look Worth Ordering

Food colour is unforgiving. A steak that reads grey or a salad that reads blue kills the appetite, so colour calibration matters at least as much as brightness. Panels with a wide colour gamut and a stable white point hold food photography accurate across the whole wall, while a screen that drifts warm or cool over a long service makes the same dish look different at lunch and at dinner. Ask for the white point to be set at commissioning and checked across panels, not just on a single sample.

Contrast shapes the composition. Dishes photographed on dark backgrounds look richer on a panel with deep black levels, which is one reason fine-pitch and COB indoor products suit premium food venues, where the black between items reads as deliberate rather than as unlit panel. In a dining room, a low-glare surface also matters more than peak output: a glossy front that mirrors a window or a pendant light into a guest's eyes is worse than a slightly dimmer, matte-finish screen.

Refresh rate protects how the wall looks on camera. Many venues film promotional content, run food videos, or appear in their own social clips, and a low-refresh panel shows banding and flicker the moment a phone points at it. A high indoor refresh rate removes that problem for both in-store footage and third-party filming. The Drive-Thru Lane Sets a Hard Specification Floor

Drive-thru is usually where the financial case is clearest, and also where the specification has the least room to flex. A screen that confirms the order visually, suggests an add-on at the right moment and shows the running total before the customer reaches the window reduces order errors and lifts the average basket, because the customer is deciding while the decision is still cheap to change. The lane also has to work in direct sun, in rain and in winter, which sets a floor a standard indoor panel cannot meet.

Specify outdoor-rated cabinets with a sealed front and enough output to stay readable against the sky, with the brightness stepping down at night so the board does not dazzle drivers approaching the order point. The ordering position also carries an audio path, and the microphone and speaker arrangement has to sit clear of the display electronics so it neither picks up noise from the panel nor interferes with it electrically. Confirm the reading height against the vehicle mix in the lane, because a board positioned for a car can be wrong for a taller van or truck.

Counter, Queue and Order-Confirmation Screens Reward a Different Build

The indoor positions look similar on a plan but pull in opposite directions. A counter menu board wants a bright, crisp wall that a queue can read from a few metres, arranged as two or three panels with tight seams so it reads as one menu. An order-confirmation or pickup screen is the opposite: small, close-viewed at roughly one to two metres, and exposed to spills, bumped trays and leaning customers, so it needs a tough front and a housing that tolerates being wiped down several times a day.

One practical rule ties the indoor positions together: the screen closest to the till should carry the fewest items. A short, bold menu speeds the decision at the point of order, while a larger dining-area wall can afford the richer photography and longer promotional loop that would slow a queue. Operators who ignore this end up with a counter board so busy that a customer cannot find the item they came in for, which costs more in lost throughput than the wall ever earns back in promotion.

Kitchen Order Displays Are a Different Product Entirely

A kitchen order display is not a menu board with a different picture on it. It is a staff tool, and it is judged on reliability and clarity rather than on photographic richness. The layout has to be simple and high-contrast so a cook can read a ticket at a glance across a hot, busy line, and the screen has to stay legible under kitchen lighting rather than compete with it. Deep black and crisp white text on a clean interface beats a darker, prettier design every time in that setting.

Reliability is the whole specification here. A counter board that fails costs some promotion; a kitchen display that fails mid-service stops orders reaching the pass, so redundancy, cable protection and a planned swap need to be part of the design from the start rather than an afterthought. Because the display sits in the harshest air in the building, the cabinet has to be sealed against grease and moisture even though the position is technically indoors, and it has to tolerate being cleaned as part of the kitchen routine rather than treated as delicate equipment.

Heat, Grease, Steam and What They Demand of the Cabinet

Kitchens are hostile to electronics. Heat, airborne grease, steam and daily cleaning with water and chemicals all attack a display designed for an office, and the damage is cumulative rather than sudden. A menu board mounted above a grill or a pass can sit in air well above normal room temperature, and a panel that runs too hot shifts colour and loses brightness quickly, so thermal management is not a detail to settle after the aesthetics. Plan ventilation and cooling for the worst day of the year, not the average.

For any position near a cooking line, specify a sealed front, filtered ventilation and a surface coating that resists grease build-up, and expect active cooling with temperature sensing so the panel can derate its own brightness before it overheats. A standard open-back cabinet will pull greasy air across its electronics and fail long before its nominal life. The same logic applies to moisture: a screen in a wash-down zone has to shed water at the surface and keep it away from the boards below.

A screen beside a cooking line has to survive all of this at once:
✅ Airborne grease that settles on and inside equipment
✅ Steam and wash-down water from the daily clean
✅ Cleaning chemicals that attack plastics and coatings
✅ Air temperature well above the dining room
✅ Knocks from trays, trolleys and staff in a tight servery
✅ A failure that lands in the middle of a service, not at a convenient hour

Cleaning Chemistry Decides the Surface You Specify

Cleaning is a design decision, not a housekeeping one. A smooth front glass with no exposed seams wipes down in seconds, while a heavily bezelled panel collects grease in every joint and around every fixing, and the joints are exactly where a cloth cannot reach. Staff will clean whatever is easiest, so the screen you specify should be built for a cloth and a food-safe spray rather than for a technician and a toolkit. A flush front also removes the ledges and recesses that trap dust in a dining room.

Ask what a cleaning regime does to the surface over a year of daily use. Some finishes tolerate food-safe chemicals and repeated wiping well; others dull, cloud or craze, and a front that has gone cloudy at month six cannot be fixed by cleaning harder. Where the screen sits within splash range of a sink, a counter or a pass, treat the cleaning chemicals as part of the environment you are specifying against, and confirm the front material and coating are chosen for that exposure rather than for a showroom.

Mounting, Cable Routes and Fitting the Screen Into the Build

In a busy servery a screen is often bumped by a tray or a trolley, so the mounting has to be as robust as the display and the cables have to be out of reach and out of sight. Brackets should lock to the structure rather than rely on a friction fit, any low-mounted panel should be protected by a barrier or set back behind the counter line, and data and power should drop into concealed routes that a passing trolley cannot snag. Decide the service side early, because a rear-service cabinet needs clear space behind the wall that the fit-out may not allow.

Power and network are best planned while the joinery and electrical work are still being drawn, not after the counter has been built. A dedicated circuit stops the display from dropping out every time a kitchen appliance switches on, and a wired network drop is more dependable than relying on Wi-Fi above a hot cooking line where signal and heat both work against you. Bringing the display into the design review early lets the screen dimensions, mounting depth and cable routes be coordinated with the millwork in a single pass instead of being retrofitted.

  • Mounting substrate and fixing method confirmed before the wall is closed up.
  • Service side chosen (front or rear) and the clearance it needs written into the plan.
  • Power circuit dedicated to the display, sized to the panel load, not shared with kitchen equipment.
  • Data route planned as a wired drop, with a fallback if the Wi-Fi surprises you.
  • Low-mounted screens protected from trolleys, trays and passing footfall.
  • Cable entries sealed at the cabinet so grease and moisture cannot follow the cable in.

Who Owns the Menu, and How It Gets Updated

A menu board that nobody can update quietly reverts to a static poster, so decide before purchase who changes the content and with what. The screen itself simply accepts a video feed from a player or a content management system; the useful questions are about the platform behind it. In a single venue, a local player may be all that is needed. In a chain, a cloud platform lets head office publish one master menu and push it to every site at once, while each location adjusts price, availability and local items for its own market.

Role-based permissions are the part that makes central control work in practice. Head office can lock core pricing and brand-critical layout while each store still manages its own daypart timing and local specials, so a promotion launches everywhere at the same moment without a phone call to every manager, and a pricing error is corrected in minutes rather than after the next print run. For a franchise group that consistency is worth more than the saving on a cheaper screen. The content scheduling guide covers daypart setup and permissions in more detail.

Content and Playback: Fewer, Bolder Items Win

Content built for a menu board is not the same as content built for a television. The transactional board at the counter should stay close to static, because a looping video with fast cuts competes with the menu the customer is trying to read and slows the very decision the screen was meant to speed up. Reserve motion and animation for a separate promotional screen that sits outside the ordering path, and let the animation support the item being promoted rather than compete with the price beside it.

Scheduling is where a modest content plan earns its keep. A venue can bring up a breakfast menu automatically, switch to an evening offer without staff intervention, run the exterior board bright through the day and dim it after dark, and darken a dining-room wall during a private function. Setting those scenes up at commissioning means the operator gets the benefit from the first trading day instead of learning the system by trial and error during a busy service, and it keeps the displays earning their place across every trading period rather than only at peak hours.

Service Access: Keeping a Dead Tile From Closing the Counter

How a screen is serviced matters more in a restaurant than in most venues, because there is rarely a closed day to work in. A front-service design lets a technician reach a module from the customer side, which suits a screen set flush into joinery or mounted where there is no space behind; a rear-service design needs a clear maintenance gap behind the wall and is the better choice when that space exists. Whichever you choose, confirm the access route at specification stage, because the choice is very hard to revisit once the counter is built.

Keep a spare module and a spare power supply on site so a dead tile on a Friday night does not cost a weekend of trading. A quick swap in a busy service area keeps the counter open, and holding matched spares from the same production batch avoids a subtle colour mismatch appearing when one panel is replaced. Window and Exterior Signage Where the Rules Allow It

Street-facing and window signage is the most light-stressed position on the site and the one most often constrained by rules you do not control. Before specifying an exterior board or a window-mounted screen, check what the lease, the landlord and the local planning rules permit on that facade, and whether there are brightness curfews or restrictions on animated content after a certain hour. A brilliant screen that breaches a consent is a liability, and the fix is far more expensive than choosing the right position at the start.

Inside the glazing the physics change again, because the screen now has to be read from outside while daylight reflects off the glass in front of it. Writing an RFQ That Comes Back With a Usable Quote

Most disappointing quotes are the result of an incomplete request, not a poor supplier. If the RFQ does not state the reading distance, the ambient light, the environment and the mounting, the supplier has to guess, and the guess is usually a generic panel that fits nothing particularly well. Give the same structured information you gathered in the zone map and every supplier answers the same question, which also makes the quotes comparable instead of merely cheap or expensive.

Include the operational side too, because it changes what gets quoted as much as the hardware does. Who updates the menu, whether one site or many, whether dayparts must switch automatically, and what service access the fit-out allows all push the quote toward one product or another. If you are comparing LED against an LCD video wall for the same position, the LED versus LCD comparison helps frame that decision before it reaches a quotation.

FAQ

Q: Are LED menu boards a better choice than LCD screens?
A: For a bright counter or a drive-thru lane, yes: LED holds up against daylight at output levels an LCD cannot match, and it does not depend on a backlight that dims over its life. In a dim, controlled dining room the advantage is smaller, and the decision usually comes down to appearance, size and whether the screen has to fight window light.
Q: How bright does a drive-thru menu board need to be?
A: Bright enough to be read from inside a car in direct sun, which sits around 5000 to 8000 nits depending on how the lane faces the sun. A board facing the afternoon sun needs the top of that range, a shaded lane can run lower, and the panel should dim automatically at night so it does not dazzle drivers at the order point.
Q: What pixel pitch is right for my screens?
A: Pitch follows the reading distance. Order confirmation and pickup screens read at one to two metres work at roughly P1.2 to P1.9, counter menu boards read at two to four metres at P1.9 to P2.6, dining walls at P2.6 to P3.9, and a drive-thru approach or forecourt board at P4 to P6. Confirm against your own measured distances.
Q: Will a screen survive grease and steam near the kitchen?
A: Not every screen will. Cooking areas push airborne grease into equipment, so any panel in that zone needs a sealed cabinet, filtered airflow and a wipe-clean surface rather than a standard open-back build. A panel without proper thermal management will shift colour and lose brightness quickly in that air, and the failure usually lands mid-service.
Q: Can one operator update every store's menu at once?
A: Yes, if the sites sit on a shared content platform. Head office can publish a master menu to every location at the same moment, then let each store adjust price, daypart timing and local items. Role-based permissions let a central team lock core pricing while local managers still handle their own specials, so a pricing error is fixed in minutes rather than at the next print run.
Q: Is a dining-room screen too bright for evening service?
A: It can be if it is specified like an outdoor board. Dining rooms want controlled output and a low-glare surface so the screen does not reflect a window or a pendant light into a guest's eyes. Match the brightness to the room's ambient light, choose a surface that suits the seating, and schedule the output to drop in the evening so the wall stays useful without dominating the table.
Q: Front-service or rear-service modules: which should I choose?
A: It depends on the space behind the wall. Front-service lets a technician reach a module from the customer side, which suits screens flush in joinery or mounted where nothing can go behind. Rear-service needs a clear maintenance gap but is easier where that space exists. Confirm the access side at specification stage, because it is very hard to change after the counter is built.
Q: What should I put in an RFQ for a restaurant display?
A: The zone map with measured reading distances, the ambient light and daylight direction at each position, notes on heat, grease, steam and wash-down, the mounting type and service access, who updates the menu and how, the power and data available, and the approval, lead-time and warranty terms you need. Give every supplier the same information so the quotes are comparable rather than merely cheap or expensive.
