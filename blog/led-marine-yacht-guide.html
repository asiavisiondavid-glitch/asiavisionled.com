By Cassie

A marine LED installation is not a showroom screen with a waterproof label. Salt spray, constant vibration, rolling motion and tight power budgets reshape every specification, from coating chemistry to cabinet weight. This guide explains how superyacht and cruise operators choose displays that survive the sea.

Market Context

Superyacht owners and cruise operators have moved LED displays from a novelty into standard fit-out. An outdoor cinema screen on the aft deck, a low-brightness information wall in the sky lounge, and a narrow bridge monitor for chart and camera feeds now appear on the specification list of most new-build vessels above a certain length. The reason is simple: a display produces light without heat, without a moving projector lamp, and without a dark room. On a yacht, all three advantages matter because space, power and service access are scarce.

Refit work drives as much of this demand as new construction. A five-year-old yacht coming in for an interior refresh is often an easier sale than a new build, because the owner already knows where a screen was awkward and where it was missing. Integrators who work the refit channel learn to design around existing cable runs, existing switchboards and existing ceiling structure rather than starting from a clean sheet.

Power is the constraint that separates marine work from land work. A vessel has a finite generator budget, and every kilowatt spent on a screen is a kilowatt unavailable for propulsion, air conditioning or galley equipment. Displays that run on the vessel's existing 12V or 24V DC bus, or that draw efficiently from an inverter at partial load, are far easier to specify than panels that demand a dedicated high-current circuit. Heat rejection is the second constraint, because a screen that dumps heat into a lounge forces the air conditioning to work harder, which feeds straight back into the generator load.

Environmental exposure defines the specification envelope. Interior spaces on a yacht still see elevated humidity and airborne salt, and a display that never leaves the saloon still needs conformal coating on its circuit boards. Exterior decks add direct spray, UV, and temperature swings between a hot engine-room side and a cool sea breeze. Any screen installed where it can be wetted must be built for it, not merely housed in a cover.

Service access is the constraint that clients underestimate most. A screen built into a saloon bulkhead may look superb on the drawing, but if a failed driver board can only be reached by removing joinery, every fault becomes a shipyard job. Marine installers therefore design a maintenance route first and mount the screen around it, leaving a removable panel, a service hatch or a swing frame so that a swap takes minutes rather than days. On a vessel in charter service, an hour of downtime has a real cost attached to it.

Finally, agree who owns the display after handover. A yacht may change captain, crew or management company during the life of the screen, and a system that only one person understands will be neglected. We recommend a simple handover pack covering the wiring, the power budget, the spare parts and the service procedure, written so a new chief engineer can pick it up without calling the installer. On a vessel that spends months away from a shipyard, that documentation is often the difference between a screen that is looked after and one that is quietly switched off.

Recommended Solutions

Match the product to the space rather than to a preferred pixel pitch. A deck cinema screen is watched from several metres away in bright ambient light, so a coarse pitch with very high output beats a fine pitch with modest output. A sky-lounge info wall is viewed from two or three metres by seated guests, so a fine pitch with a matte finish and controlled brightness serves better. A bridge or wheelhouse monitor is a working instrument, so colour accuracy, response time and low latency matter more than sheer brightness.

Whichever class you choose, insist on three construction details. First, conformal coating across the driver board and power supply so moisture cannot bridge fine traces. Second, marine-grade stainless or treated fasteners and a cabinet finish that will not bloom in salt air. Third, a rigid mounting interface that tolerates flex without stressing solder joints. A display can have excellent specifications and still fail in a season if any one of these is skipped.

Cabinet weight deserves its own line in the specification. Everything on a yacht has to be carried aboard, lifted through a hatch or a sliding door, and positioned by hand in a space with no crane access. A screen that is easy to build on land can be impossible to install below deck. Lighter cabinets reduce not only the handling problem but also the structural load on a bulkhead that was never designed for a hanging mass, and they make the difference between a two-person lift and a rigging contractor.

Consider the audio and video chain at the same time as the panel. Marine lounges typically run a dedicated media server, and the screen has to accept that source cleanly without an adapter chain that introduces its own failure points. Where the vessel operates in a humid environment, the rack itself needs ventilation and a stable supply, and the control interface should be something a steward can operate without training rather than a menu buried three levels deep in an installer's app.

FAQs

Q: Are standard LED displays suitable for marine use?
A: Not as delivered. A conventional indoor panel will corrode at its board traces and fasteners once salt air and humidity reach it. Marine use requires conformal coating on the electronics, treated or stainless hardware, and a cabinet finish rated for salt exposure. Asia Vision builds these into the marine specification rather than adding them as an aftermarket spray.
Q: What voltage do marine LED displays use?
A: Most yacht installations run on the vessel's 12V or 24V DC bus, so we supply DC-input power supplies that avoid an extra conversion stage. Larger cruise ships and shore-connected vessels typically use standard AC input, and we size the power supply around the vessel's real generator headroom rather than the panel's peak rating alone.
Q: Can yacht LED displays operate in saltwater environments?
A: Yes, with the right build. Exterior units use an IP65-rated front and sealed cabinet so wind-driven spray cannot enter. Interior units in a saloon or sky lounge still need coated boards because salt is carried through the air conditioning, but they do not need the full exterior enclosure.
Q: What screen size fits on a yacht?
A: Size is set by the mounting surface and the viewing distance, not by a fixed product list. Small lounge walls suit panels from roughly 55 inches upward, while an aft-deck cinema screen can exceed 150 inches and be assembled from cabinets. We design custom widths and heights so the screen fits the structure instead of the structure being modified to fit the screen.
Q: How do you handle heat and power limits on board?
A: We calculate the display's real average draw, not just its peak figure, and match it to the vessel's available headroom. Choosing an efficient power supply and a cabinet that spreads heat rather than concentrating it keeps the load on the air conditioning low, which matters on a boat where cooling capacity is shared with the rest of the interior.
Q: What about vibration and motion?
A: Rolling and slamming put repeated stress on solder joints and connectors. We specify rigid cabinet frames, locking connectors and mounting brackets that clamp the panel firmly without over-constraining it, so movement is absorbed by the mounting rather than by the module itself.
