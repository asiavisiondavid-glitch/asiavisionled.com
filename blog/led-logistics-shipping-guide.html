By Cassie

An LED display is a high-value, fragile, volumetric cargo, and it behaves differently in transit from most industrial goods. The panels are delicate on the face and heavy for their size, the crates are bulky relative to their weight, and a single handling mistake can turn a working wall into a replacement order. Freight planning therefore starts long before the shipment leaves the factory.

Logistics is also where a project schedule is most easily lost. A screen that is ready in production can still miss its installation window because a certificate was not raised in time, because a port is congested, or because the crates were booked on a sailing that does not match the site programme. This guide covers the shipping considerations that decide a delivery, the practices that keep cargo intact, and the commercial and support arrangements that surround an LED order.

What Are the Key Considerations?

The first consideration is that LED cargo is rated by volume rather than by weight. Crates are built to protect a delicate face rather than to compress, so a shipment occupies considerably more container space than its mass would suggest, and freight cost follows the volumetric measure. That has a practical consequence for planning: consolidating several orders into one shipment reduces cost per unit, while splitting a project into phases multiplies the handling events and the freight bill. The right answer depends on whether the site can accept a single large delivery or needs the wall in stages.

The second consideration is the fragility profile of the cargo. A fine-pitch module has a delicate surface that must not bear load, while a coarse-pitch outdoor cabinet is heavy and can damage anything it moves against. Crating therefore has to prevent both compression on the face and movement inside the crate, which is why cabinets are braced and foam-protected rather than simply boxed. Damage in transit is almost always a packaging or handling issue rather than a manufacturing defect, and it is largely preventable by correct crating and by a freight route that minimises transfers.

The third consideration is customs and conformity documentation, which is the most common cause of a consignment being held after it arrives. Conformity marks such as CE, FCC or RoHS certification, a certificate of origin, a commercial invoice and a packing list all have to be correct and consistent, and markets with a mandatory registration scheme require an approval that must be scheduled into production rather than raised after the shipment has sailed. What Are the Best Practices?

Book the freight against the installation programme rather than against the production date. A shipment that arrives a fortnight before the site is ready creates a storage problem for a fragile cargo, and one that arrives a fortnight late leaves a crew standing. Working backwards from the date the first cabinets are needed on the mounting frame, allowing for production, sailing or flight time, port handling and inland delivery, produces a schedule that can actually be met. Markets with a certification requirement add a step that should be mapped on the same timeline.

Insure the cargo for its full value and inspect it before signing. Transit damage on a delicate cargo is not rare enough to be left to chance, and the full value of a large wall is a significant exposure relative to the cost of a policy. The practical discipline at the receiving end is to inspect the crates for external damage before signing the delivery note, photograph anything that looks wrong, and note the damage on the receiving document. Signing a clean receipt for a damaged crate removes most of the leverage a claim depends on.

Minimise transfers and keep the cargo dry. Every additional handling event is an opportunity for an impact, and every additional port is an opportunity for a delay. A direct sailing or flight is worth a premium on a high-value screen, and the saving from a cheaper multi-leg route is small compared with the cost of a damaged panel or a missed installation. Where cargo is held in a container across a long voyage, a desiccant load and a moisture barrier protect the electronics from the condensation that temperature swings produce.

Prepare the receiving site as carefully as the shipment. Confirm that the delivery vehicle can reach the unloading point, that there is equipment capable of moving the crates, and that there is secure, dry storage if the cabinets are not being installed immediately. Crates should be opened in a clean area rather than on a building site, and panels should be allowed to reach the ambient temperature of the installation environment before they are energised, particularly on an outdoor project where cold electronics meeting warm humid air is a real condensation risk.

FAQ: Buyer Questions

Q: Is sea freight or air freight better for an LED display order?
A: Sea freight is the default for an LED order because the cargo is large and bulky, and the cost per cubic metre is a fraction of air freight. Air freight is worth considering for urgent requirements such as a single replacement cabinet for an operating screen, where the cost is justified by restoring a revenue-generating installation rather than by the price of transport. Rail is a middle option for some destinations and is often used where a schedule sits between the two. Asia Vision quotes production at 15 to 20 working days, which is the variable that most often decides whether the freight mode can be the economical one.
Q: How should LED panels be packaged for international shipping?
A: Cabinets are crated rather than boxed, with the frame braced to prevent movement and the module face protected from any load. The face of a fine-pitch module is delicate and must not bear weight, so crates are designed to transfer load through the structure rather than through the panel, and cabinets are separated by foam or dividers so they cannot contact each other in transit. Power and data accessories belonging to the series are packed in the same shipment, and the packing list itemises everything so that a short delivery is identified when the crate is opened rather than on the day of commissioning.
Q: Who arranges customs clearance for an LED display import?
A: Clearance is normally handled by a freight forwarder acting for the importer, using the commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin and conformity certificates supplied with the shipment. The important preparation is that these documents are consistent and complete before dispatch, because a discrepancy between an invoice and a packing list is one of the most common reasons a consignment sits at port. Markets that require a local registration or a pre-shipment inspection need that step scheduled into the production timeline, and Asia Vision prepares the destination documentation with the order rather than after it.
Q: What insurance should cover an LED display shipment?
A: Insure the cargo for its full value from the factory to the installation site, and confirm that the policy covers the specific risks of the route and the mode of transport. A screen is a high-value item and the loss of a container of panels is a material exposure. The practical step that most often determines whether a claim succeeds is the inspection at delivery: check the crates for external damage before signing the receipt, photograph anything that appears damaged, and record the finding on the receiving document. Signing an unqualified receipt for a damaged crate makes a claim difficult to pursue.
Q: How long does shipping take from China to a destination market?
A: The schedule is the sum of production, inland transport to the port, the sailing or flight, port handling and inland delivery, and it varies with the destination and the route chosen. Production is quoted at 15 to 20 working days, and sea freight adds a transit period that depends on the lane and the number of ports of call, while air freight is measured in days rather than weeks. This is why the freight decision should be made against the installation date rather than the production date, and why a phased project often ships in several consignments to keep each installation phase supplied.
Q: What should I check when the crates arrive on site?
A: Inspect the crates externally before signing the delivery note, looking for crushed corners, punctures, water staining or evidence that a crate has fallen, and photograph anything that concerns you. Once the receipt is signed, open the crates in a clean, dry area rather than on an active construction site, and check the contents against the packing list before the cabinets are moved to the mounting position. Allow the cabinets to reach the ambient temperature of the installation environment before energising them, especially on an outdoor project where cold panels exposed to warm humid air can develop condensation on the electronics.

Import Logistics and Market Entry

Importing an LED display is a documentation exercise as much as a transport one, and the markets that cause the most delay are those with a mandatory conformity scheme. The commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin and conformity certificates have to be consistent with one another and with what is physically in the container, because a mismatch is the single most common reason a consignment is held for inspection. Asia Vision prepares destination documentation with the order and configures the cabinet for the destination voltage and frequency at the factory, so the shipment arrives ready to connect rather than awaiting a modification.

Where a market requires a local registration, a pre-shipment inspection or a certification mark that is issued locally, that step has to be scheduled into the production timeline rather than added after the goods are ready. Making that decision at the order stage, rather than at the port, is what separates a delivery that lands on schedule from one that absorbs a fortnight of storage and demurrage. Countries with an established approval route are handled through the appropriate channels so the file is complete when the consignment arrives.

On the destination side, the inland leg is often the least planned part of the journey and the one that produces the most damage. Confirm that the delivery vehicle can reach the unloading point, that lifting equipment is available for the crated weight, and that a secure, dry storage area exists if the cabinets are not being installed the same week they arrive. Delivery terms should be agreed in writing with the forwarder and the site so that responsibility at the handover point is unambiguous, and the inspection before signing the receipt is the moment that determines whether a transit claim is recoverable.

Equipment Comparison

Comparing suppliers on shipping is largely a comparison of how well they plan for the cargo surviving the journey. The questions worth asking are whether crate dimensions and weights are published before dispatch, whether accessories ship with the panels or separately, whether the conformity documentation is prepared by the factory or left to the importer, and whether the supplier can credibly split a project into phased shipments matched to an installation programme. A supplier who can answer those questions in figures makes the freight a planned line item; one who cannot makes it a contingency.

The second comparison point is packing repeatability, which matters most to a rental business. Cabinets that ship in a reusable flight case with a fixed pack configuration can be reloaded by the touring crew without repacking, while cabinets that arrive in one-way packaging are rebuilt for every shipment. Logistics for a US Delivery

Logistics into the United States adds one leg that does not appear on shorter routes: the inland delivery from the port or airport to the site, which on a large country can be several days of trucking.

Three points belong in the plan. The port of entry, because it decides the inland distance and therefore the delivery cost and time. The entry, which is made per arrival with its own brokerage, duty assessment and record-keeping obligation on the importer of record. And the inland leg, which should be quoted separately so that a change in one part does not silently alter another.

Two practical points. Where the installation is phased, each shipment carries its own brokerage and inland cost, so a landed cost calculated on one consignment understates a staged programme. And where the site is ready before the goods, storage near the port or at the site becomes a real line rather than an assumption.
