What is a stadium LED display tender? A stadium LED display tender is the procurement process a venue uses to buy scoreboards, big screens, and perimeter boards under published rules and scoring criteria. It differs from ordinary display purchasing because every screen may appear on broadcast. This 2026 guide explains how to write the specification, score the bids, and protect the venue.

A stadium LED display tender is not a standard equipment purchase. The screens sit inside the broadcast frame, they must survive weather and crowd contact, and they carry sponsorship revenue that depends on them working every match day. The tender has to reflect all three.

Venues often begin with a price-led approach and discover later that the specification did not capture the performance that matters. Building the tender around broadcast evidence, weather protection, and service response produces a screen that still works in year five.

Writing the Specification for a Stadium LED Display Tender

Write the specification as outcomes with tests attached. State the refresh rate with the camera and shutter speed, the brightness under the measured site conditions, the viewing distances for each screen position, and the ingress rating for the venue's weather exposure.

Separate the display types rather than treating the venue as one requirement. A big screen, a scoreboard, a perimeter board, and a concourse screen each have different requirements for pitch, brightness, and protection, and a single specification across all of them wastes money on the simple ones.

Screen PositionPrimary RequirementTest Method
Main big screenBrightness and pitch for the standMeasurement and viewing test

Concourse screensDurability and low maintenanceIngress and impact assessment

Exterior facadeHigh brightness and sealingIngress rating and brightness check

Attach the test method to every requirement in the tender document itself. A requirement without a test is a preference, and a preference cannot be scored or enforced. The test method also tells bidders what evidence they must submit, which makes the bids comparable.

Broadcast Evidence in a Stadium LED Display Tender

The perimeter and big screens will appear on broadcast throughout the season, and banding is the most visible failure a venue can suffer. Require a camera test at the shutter speeds the broadcast production uses, and ask for the driver IC model and scan rate in the bid.

Check the evidence before the award rather than after delivery. A bid that states a refresh figure without footage risks delivering a screen that bands under stadium lighting, and the venue cannot afford to discover that during a televised fixture.

Broadcast evidence to require:
✅ Camera test at production shutter speeds
✅ Driver IC make and model named
✅ Scan rate stated
✅ Test content including fast motion
✅ Footage submitted with the bid

Evaluation Criteria and Scoring

Publish the criteria and weightings before the bids arrive, and score every offer against the same questions. Price is one criterion among several, and the weightings should reflect the venue's real exposures, starting with broadcast performance and service response.

CriterionWhat It MeasuresTypical Weight
Technical complianceMeets every stated requirement25 to 35 percent
Broadcast evidenceCamera test and driver detail15 to 25 percent
Price and whole-life costCost across the screen life20 to 30 percent
Service and sparesSupport across the season15 to 20 percent
Installation approachRigging and access plan5 to 15 percent
Delivery programmeFits the fixture calendar5 to 10 percent

Score technical compliance as pass or fail on the mandatory requirements, then score the remaining criteria on a defined scale. A bid that fails a mandatory requirement is rejected regardless of price, which prevents a cheap non-compliant offer from entering the final comparison.

Installation and the Fixture Calendar

Stadium installation has to fit around fixtures, and the tender should require the bidder to set out how the work will be sequenced. A screen that takes a stadium out of use for several weeks costs far more in lost matchday revenue than the hardware itself.

Ask for a phased plan that separates structural work from display installation, so the structural preparation can happen during the close season and the screens can be commissioned before the first fixture. Require the bidder to state the access requirements and the crew size.

Installation plan to require:
✅ Phased programme around fixtures
✅ Access and rigging requirements stated
✅ Commissioning before the first fixture
✅ Contingency if the programme slips
✅ Testing schedule with the venue

Service and Spares Across a Season

A venue cannot wait days for a spare module, so the tender should require a spare parts package and a response time that matches the fixture calendar. Require the bidder to state what spares are held, where they are held, and how quickly a technician can attend.

Screen the service commitment against the match schedule. A response time measured in business days is meaningless for a venue that plays at weekends and in the evening, and the tender should say so explicitly rather than leaving it to negotiation after award.

Training venue staff to perform basic swaps changes the economics of the whole contract. A venue that can replace a module in twenty minutes rarely misses a fixture, while one that depends on an external technician will eventually darken a section during a broadcast.

Whole-Life Cost in a Stadium LED Display Tender

Score whole-life cost rather than purchase price. Energy, service, spares, and downtime vary between suppliers, and a lower price with higher running costs can lose across a decade of operation. Ask bidders for the inputs needed to model total cost.

Include the cost of any fixture disruption in the model. A screen that needs scaffolding and a closure for every repair carries a cost that never appears in the purchase price, while a front-serviceable design removes it entirely.

Sponsorship and Content Systems

The display is a sponsorship asset, so the tender should require a control system that handles scheduled advertising rotations, live score updates, and instant switching between content sources. Sponsors pay for guaranteed exposure, which means the system must prove that each advert ran.

Require a playout log as part of the deliverable. The log is the evidence behind a sponsorship contract and the first diagnostic tool when a fault appears, and a supplier who cannot provide one leaves the venue without proof of its own obligations.

Contract Terms and Acceptance

Set the acceptance test as a contract condition. The venue should not pay the final sum until the screens pass the same tests the tender required, which ties the award to the outcome the specification described rather than to a promise made during bidding.

Document the test results with measurements, photographs, and camera footage, and keep them with the contract. The same evidence supports a warranty claim later and gives the venue a baseline for judging degradation across the years.

Common Failures in Stadium Tenders

  • Specifying one requirement for all screen positions
  • Accepting a refresh figure without camera footage
  • Scoring price so heavily that broadcast evidence is ignored
  • Setting a service response that ignores the fixture calendar
  • Omitting the installation programme from the evaluation
  • Awarding without an acceptance test in the contract

Each failure costs the venue either money or reputation, and the second is harder to recover. A stadium LED display tender that captures broadcast performance, service response, and installation sequencing produces a screen that still works when the sponsorship contracts are renewed.

A venue that documents the tender criteria and the evaluation will find the next procurement faster, because the reasoning behind the specification is available to reuse rather than reconstruct.

The strongest bids come from suppliers who treat a stadium LED display tender as an opportunity to sharpen their evidence file rather than as an isolated transaction with no lasting value.

The record also protects the venue when the sponsorship contracts are renegotiated, because the performance evidence that justified the award is the same evidence that supports the guarantee.

Venues that treat the tender as a long-term operating decision rather than a one-off purchase avoid the cycle of replacing screens before their time.

Tendering for a US Sports Venue

A stadium or arena project in North America is usually publicly funded or publicly owned, which brings a formal procurement route and a documented evaluation.

Three requirements recur. The certification file at submittal, because fire and electrical requirements are conditions of installation. An origin condition where the funding carries one, checked against the manufacturing country at evaluation rather than after award. And documented redundancy and uptime commitments, because a venue screen carries match-critical content and an outage is an operational incident.

Two practical points for a bidder. Confirm the service access method, since a screen mounted above a stand or behind a fascia is often unreachable from the rear and the closure cost then dominates the maintenance case. And quote the delivered cost to the site on a named Incoterm, because the inland leg on a US venue can be a significant share of the delivery.

FAQ

Q: What should a stadium LED display tender specify?
A: Specify each screen position separately with outcomes and test methods: refresh with a camera test, brightness under measured site conditions, viewing distances, and ingress rating. A single requirement for all screens wastes money.
Q: Why is broadcast evidence important in the tender?
A: The screens appear on broadcast throughout the season, and banding is the most visible failure a venue can suffer. Require a camera test at production shutter speeds and the driver IC model before the award.
Q: How should service be scored?
A: Score response time against the fixture calendar, require spare modules held at or near the venue, and include out-of-hours cover. A response measured in business days is meaningless for a venue that plays at weekends.
Q: Should the venue train its own staff?
A: Yes. Training venue staff to swap a module changes the economics of the contract, because a venue that can repair a section in twenty minutes rarely misses a fixture while one dependent on an external technician will eventually go dark.
Q: How do I make the award defensible?
A: Publish the criteria and weightings in advance, score every claim against named evidence, and document the reasoning. Tie the final payment to an acceptance test written into the contract, as any stadium LED display tender should.
