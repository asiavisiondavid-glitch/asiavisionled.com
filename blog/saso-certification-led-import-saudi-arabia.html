SASO certification is mandatory for LED display products imported into Saudi Arabia, and it operates through the online SABER platform. Importers must register products, obtain a Product Certificate of Conformity, and secure a Shipment Certificate of Conformity before customs will release the goods. This guide explains the process from the supplier's first documentation submission through to clearance at Jeddah or Dammam.

Understanding the two certificates and when each is needed is the key to avoiding port delays. A product certificate covers the model; a shipment certificate covers each individual consignment. Treating them as interchangeable is the most common mistake new importers make.

Step-by-Step SASO Certification Process

The process begins with the supplier assembling the technical documentation for the product series. This includes test reports, wiring diagrams, component lists, product images and the manufacturer's declaration. Completeness at this stage determines how quickly everything that follows moves, because a certification body cannot assess against SASO and IEC standards with gaps in the file.

The certification body then reviews the submission against the applicable LED display standards. For a genuinely new product series, in-country testing may be required, which adds time and requires a physical sample. Products that already hold recognised international test reports generally move faster, because those reports can serve as the technical basis for the Saudi assessment.

Once approved, the supplier or importer obtains a Product Certificate of Conformity, the PCOC, which is registered on SABER and remains valid for a defined period depending on the product's risk category. The PCOC is the model-level approval and must exist before any shipment certificate can be raised against that model.

Before each consignment ships, the importer registers the shipment on SABER to obtain a Shipment Certificate of Conformity, the SCOC. This is the document Saudi customs actually demands at clearance, and it must be issued for the specific shipment. Without a valid SCOC the container cannot clear, regardless of how many other certificates the supplier holds.

Registration on SABER requires an account linked to the importer's commercial registration, so the paperwork has to be raised in the name of the party importing the goods. Importers new to the platform should allow time for account setup and for linking the supplier's product certificate before the first shipment, because these administrative steps cannot be completed while the vessel is already at sea.

It also pays to match the certificate to the exact products in the container. A PCOC covers a defined model or series, and shipping a cabinet that the certificate does not name will trigger a rejection at the port. Where a supplier offers several display series, each may require its own certificate, and a mixed container therefore needs the certificates for every model inside it to be in order.

Asia Vision pre-certifies its product ranges so that Saudi importers can move quickly once a purchase order is placed. Having the product-level approval already in place converts what could be a multi-week bottleneck into a documentation step measured in days.

Documentation quality has a direct commercial effect on this process. A supplier who provides a complete, well-organised technical file shortens the assessment, while one who sends partial records repeatedly extends it. Buyers should therefore treat the completeness of the documentation pack as part of the supplier evaluation, not an afterthought once the order is placed, because the certification timeline sits on the critical path between payment and delivery.

It is also worth building the certification schedule into the project plan from the outset. A new product series that needs in-country testing can add several weeks before a single container is cleared, so a project with a fixed opening date should begin the SASO work well before the purchase order is finalised. Where the supplier already holds a PCOC for the model in question, that lead time largely disappears, which is one reason pre-certified product ranges are attractive to importers working against a deadline.

Importers should keep the SABER account and all associated records in the company's own name, so that the certification history belongs to the importer rather than to a third party. This makes renewals and future shipments straightforward and avoids dependency on an intermediary.

SASO vs Other Certifications

SASO sits alongside, rather than replaces, the international certifications a manufacturer already holds. CE, IEC and similar reports describe a product's technical compliance, and they are accepted as the technical foundation for a SASO assessment. They do not, however, exempt a product from the Saudi process, because SASO certification via SABER is a separate regulatory requirement enforced at the border.

The practical relationship is straightforward: strong IEC or CE test reports make the SASO assessment faster and cheaper because less new testing is needed, but the PCOC and SCOC must still be obtained. A product with excellent European paperwork but no SCOC will not clear Saudi customs.

Regional differences matter too. Neighbouring Gulf markets run their own conformity schemes, and a product moving from the UAE into Saudi Arabia still needs the Saudi documentation. Importers serving multiple Gulf countries should plan certification as a market-by-market exercise rather than assume one approval covers the region.

It is also worth understanding what SASO certification is not. It is not a quality ranking and it is not a warranty of performance; it is evidence that the product meets the applicable safety and standards requirements for the market. Buyers still need to assess brightness, pixel pitch, refresh rate and build quality on their own terms, because a compliant product can still be the wrong product for a given installation.

Because SASO validity is tiered by product risk category, a low-risk accessory may enjoy a longer certificate life than a complex outdoor display. Buyers should confirm the validity period attached to their specific series and diary the renewal date, since an expired PCOC blocks every subsequent SCOC application.

Shipping Routes & Costs

Saudi Arabia is served by two principal container ports and one meaningful air corridor. Jeddah on the Red Sea handles the western population centres including Mecca and Medina and is the natural gateway for the largest concentration of commercial and religious tourism projects. Dammam on the Gulf serves the Eastern Province and its industrial and energy sector.

Transit from Shenzhen to Jeddah is typically shorter than to Dammam because of the routing, and Jeddah is therefore often the default choice for importers whose receivers are anywhere in the west or centre of the country. For a receiver in Riyadh, either port can work, with the decision turning on inland trucking distance and current port congestion.

Air freight to Riyadh or Jeddah is reserved for samples, urgent replacement modules or event deadlines. The premium is significant for bulky display hardware, so it is not a production channel, but for a single critical cabinet it can be the difference between a functioning installation and a dark screen.

Freight rates to Saudi Arabia fluctuate with capacity, fuel and seasonal demand around Ramadan and the Hajj period, when vessel space tightens. Importers with fixed deadlines should book early and hold a buffer, rather than assume that a rate quoted in a quiet month will hold during peak season. Consolidating multiple project orders into one container also improves the economics considerably.

Packing deserves the same attention as the sailing schedule. LED cabinets are large, rigid and easily damaged by poor stowage, so a supplier who understands export packing will brace the load, protect cabinet corners and use flight cases for rental hardware. Damage discovered after clearance is difficult to claim against a carrier, which makes correct packing at origin the most reliable protection.

Finally, importers should confirm who bears risk at each stage of the journey. The Incoterm chosen, whether FOB, CIF or delivered duty paid, determines where the buyer's exposure begins and who arranges insurance. Agreeing this in writing before booking avoids the situation where a damaged consignment falls between two parties with no clear responsibility.

FAQ

Q: How long does SASO certification take?
A: Initial product certification typically runs four to eight weeks. Once the PCOC is in place, each shipment certificate is normally issued within a few working days via SABER. Asia Vision pre-certifies its ranges so that shipments can move as soon as an order is confirmed.
Q: Can I use IEC or CE certification instead of SASO?
A: IEC and CE reports are accepted as the technical basis for a Saudi assessment, but SASO certification through SABER is still mandatory. Products cannot clear Saudi customs without a valid SCOC, whatever international certificates they hold.
Q: What is the difference between a PCOC and an SCOC?
A: The PCOC is a model-level product certificate valid for one to three years. The SCOC is issued per shipment and is the document customs requires at the border. Both are raised through the SABER platform, and the PCOC must exist first.
Q: Does a product stored in a free zone need SASO certification?
A: Goods held in a free zone purely for re-export may not require full Saudi certification, but any product entering the mainland market does. Importers should confirm the intended end use before deciding how much certification to obtain.
Q: What happens if a shipment arrives without an SCOC?
A: The container cannot clear customs and will incur storage and demurrage charges while the certificate is obtained. This is why shipment certification must be treated as a pre-departure task rather than a post-arrival one.
