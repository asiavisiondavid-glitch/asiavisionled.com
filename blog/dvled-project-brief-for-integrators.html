By David

A dvLED brief that gives an integrator the room, the content, the hours of use and the service expectation will produce proposals that can be compared; a brief that gives a size and a budget will produce prices that cannot. The difference is decided before anyone quotes.

This guide covers what an end user should put in the brief, what to ask during selection, and how to compare proposals that arrive in different formats.

What Belongs in the Brief

Five items make proposals comparable.

The space, with dimensions and photographs. The viewing distance, nearest and farthest, measured rather than estimated. The content, described specifically — live data, video conference, presentation, imagery — because it drives the specification more than the room does. Hours of use per day and days per week. And the service expectation, in terms of how quickly a fault has to be resolved and whether the building has anyone to do it.

Two further items prevent later confusion. State the budget envelope, including installation and service, so the proposal is scoped to it rather than to a wish list. And state whether the building work — power, structure, access — is inside or outside the contract.

Questions That Separate Proposals

The questions below are the ones whose answers vary most between integrators, and they are not usually covered by an equipment quotation.

What is the response time if a module fails, and where are the spares held? What is the brightness headroom over the measured room light level, given that output declines over the life of the screen? Who witnesses the acceptance test, and what are the pass criteria? What is the delivered cost to the building, on a named delivery term, including installation? And what happens to the price if the site is not ready on the planned date?

Comparing Proposals That Look Different

Proposals arrive in different formats, and the comparison is usually being made between a cheaper option described in detail and a more expensive one described in general.

Three normalisations make the comparison fair. Compare the delivered cost to site rather than the equipment price, because freight, installation and the service arrangement are frequently excluded. Compare against the same specification, meaning the same pitch and brightness, since a proposal that has quietly reduced either is not cheaper, it is different. And compare the service arrangement as a line, because a lower equipment price with no local spares can be the more expensive decision over the term.

Where the Manufacturer Sits

During selection it is worth understanding which manufacturer sits behind each proposal, because the integrator’s service commitment is only as good as the supply behind it.

Two documents tell you most of it. The cut sheet shows whether the specification is actually achievable, and the certification file shows whether the installation will pass inspection where a code applies. An integrator who can produce both without delay is working with a manufacturer who has them.

FAQ

Q: What should be in a dvLED brief?
A: The space and its dimensions, the nearest and farthest viewing distance, the content that will be shown, the hours of use, the service expectation, the budget envelope, and whether building work is included in the contract.
Q: How do I compare dvLED proposals fairly?
A: Compare delivered cost to site on the same delivery term, against the same pitch and brightness, with the service arrangement treated as a separate line. A lower equipment price with no local spares or installation is not a like-for-like comparison.
Q: Should I choose the lowest price?
A: Not on the equipment line alone. Over a ten-year installation the service arrangement, the local availability of spares and the power consumption all carry cost, and a cheaper wall can be the more expensive decision.
Q: What certifications should the proposal evidence?
A: Whatever the local building code requires for a permanently installed display, together with the equipment authorisation for any device with radio components. Ask for the certification file at the proposal stage rather than at installation.
