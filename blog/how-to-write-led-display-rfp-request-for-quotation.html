By David

What is an LED display RFQ? A request for quotation (RFQ) is a written document that asks factories to quote the same LED display specification under the same commercial terms. A clear LED display RFQ produces comparable prices, prevents hidden exclusions, and gives buyers a fair basis for awarding an order. This 2026 template guide explains every section a factory needs to quote accurately.

Most confusing factory quotes are the buyer's fault, not the factory's. When each supplier receives a different verbal description, each quote has a different specification, and comparison becomes guesswork. A structured RFQ solves this by defining the screen, the components, the delivery terms, and the documents in writing before any pricing conversation starts.

Why a Written RFQ Protects You

A written RFQ creates a binding baseline. Once a factory quotes against your document, the model number, pixel pitch, brightness, and included components are fixed. If the factory later changes the specification, you have the original RFQ to prove the change. Buyers who skip this step discover the value of documentation only during a dispute.

Sections Every LED Display RFQ Needs

  • Project background and installation environment
  • Screen size, resolution, and pixel pitch
  • Brightness, refresh rate, and color requirements
  • Cabinet type, weight limit, and service access
  • Included components: power supply, receiver, control system
  • Certification and documentation requirements
  • Quantity, spare parts, and packaging
  • Delivery terms, destination, and timeline
  • Payment terms and warranty conditions
  • Quote validity and response format

What Factories Answer Badly

Watch for three weak responses: no model number, a specification sheet that differs from the quote, and exclusions hidden in the notes. A factory that cannot answer a simple RFQ with model numbers will be even harder to manage during production. Ask every factory to respond in the same table format so differences are visible.

Scoring rule for RFQ responses:
✅ Completeness: all 10 sections answered
✅ Specification match: same model and components
✅ Compliance: certification files included
✅ Commercial terms: freight, payment, warranty stated
✅ Communication: technical questions answered clearly

After responses arrive, verify certification in UL Product iQ or Intertek's ETL directory and compare line by line using the quote comparison guide. The factory with the cleanest RFQ response usually becomes the most predictable production partner.

RFQ Mistakes That Create Bad Quotes

The most expensive mistake is sending a vague screen size without environment, content type, or delivery destination. Factories then quote different pixel pitches, brightness levels, and component sets, and the buyer compares numbers that mean different products. The second mistake is changing the specification after quotes arrive, which gives factories an excuse to adjust pricing.

  • No screen size or pixel pitch specified
  • No environment: indoor, outdoor, sunlight
  • No destination port or delivery term
  • No certification requirement listed
  • No warranty or spare parts requirement
  • Specification changed after quotes arrive

Defining the Screen Specification

State the viewing distance and the content plan so the pixel pitch can be evaluated. A control room that shows dashboards from two meters needs fine pitch; a billboard seen from 50 meters does not. Give the factory the decision context, then require it to confirm the exact resolution and LED brand in its response.

List every included component: power supply, receiver card, sender, video processor, cables, and control software. Ask for a unit price per spare module, power supply, and receiver card. Buyers who leave these items out discover them at the invoice stage.

Defining Delivery and Acceptance Terms

Write the delivery term as a requirement, not a suggestion: EXW, FOB, CIF, or DDP, plus the destination port or address. DDP is usually best for first-time importers because the factory manages customs and inland delivery. Acceptance terms should name the inspection point, the documentation required, and the payment release condition.

Acceptance criteria belong in the RFQ so the factory prices them correctly. For US projects, require UL/ETL and FCC files for the exact model. For brightness, state a measured range with a 5-10% tolerance. For quality, state the dead pixel threshold and the calibration requirement.

How to Compare RFQ Responses

Build a comparison table with the same fields for every factory: model number, resolution, brightness, refresh rate, included components, certification, freight term, warranty, spares, and delivery date. A factory that answers every field with a concrete number is ready to produce; one that writes marketing language is not.

RFQ response scorecard:
✅ Every section answered with model numbers
✅ Certification file numbers provided
✅ Component brands and quantities listed
✅ Delivery and payment terms in writing
✅ Deviations explained, not hidden

Send the RFQ to three to five qualified factories and request a 30-45 day quote validity. After responses arrive, verify each factory's certification in the public directories and compare line by line. The supplier selection guide and the quote comparison checklist give you the full workflow.

Defining Quality and Warranty Terms

Quality terms protect you after the quote becomes an order. Define the dead pixel threshold, brightness tolerance, calibration requirement, and inspection point in the RFQ. Factories price to their quality level, so a buyer who demands measurement reports receives a different quote than one who accepts a brochure. Make the requirement visible at the RFQ stage and the factory cannot claim surprise later.

Warranty language belongs in the same document. Ask for the warranty period, the included spare parts percentage, the replacement process, and who pays freight on warranty returns. A factory that writes these terms clearly is confident; one that avoids the question is leaving room to negotiate after payment.

Using an RFQ Template Across Projects

Build one RFQ template and reuse it with small changes for each project. The template should contain the ten required sections, the acceptance criteria, and the commercial terms. Reuse creates a consistent evaluation process and makes it easy to compare suppliers across different projects over time.

RFQ template fields:
✅ Project name, environment, and date
✅ Screen size, resolution, and pixel pitch
✅ Brightness, refresh, and color requirements
✅ Components, spares, and packaging
✅ Certification and documentation
✅ Delivery term, destination, and timeline
✅ Payment, warranty, and acceptance terms

RFQ Language That Keeps Factories Honest

Use exact language instead of adjectives. Instead of writing "high brightness", write "measured 6,000 nits at 25 degrees after 30 minutes warm-up, tolerance 5-10%". Instead of writing "good warranty", write "3 years parts and labor, 3% spare modules included, buyer pays freight on replacements". Precision makes the response measurable and the factory accountable.

Finally, ask factories to list every exclusion. A quote that says "excludes steel structure and installation" is honest; a quote that simply omits them is a trap. When exclusions are written, you can compare total project cost instead of comparing screen prices that leave half the budget invisible.

A good RFQ also gives the factory the commercial context it needs to quote fairly: estimated quantity, expected delivery window, and whether the project is a one-time purchase or a recurring program. Factories price differently for one wall versus a distributor program, and hiding the context only produces a quote that does not match the real order.

When responses arrive, check the validity period and the date on every document. A factory that quotes with an expired price list or a certificate for another company is showing weak document control. Document control predicts production control, so treat it as part of the quality evaluation rather than an administrative detail.

The RFQ should also state how responses will be evaluated so factories know what matters. When buyers publish the scoring method, suppliers invest in the areas that count and stop guessing. This transparency produces better responses and makes the final award decision easier to explain to internal stakeholders.

A short scoring table with weights for price, specification match, delivery, and support turns subjective impressions into a decision the whole team can review.

A well-written RFQ is a procurement asset that saves time in every later project because suppliers already understand your requirements and respond with fewer questions.

Use the same template structure for every supplier so responses stay comparable.

FAQ

Q: What is the difference between an RFQ and an RFP?
A: An RFQ focuses on pricing a defined specification. An RFP is broader and evaluates approaches, technical proposals, and partners. LED hardware buying usually starts with an RFQ, while large system projects may use an RFP.
Q: How many factories should receive my LED display RFQ?
A: Three to five qualified factories is a good balance. Too few gives no comparison, and too many creates unmanageable variance. Shortlist factories that already have relevant certification and reference projects.
Q: Should I include my budget in the RFQ?
A: Usually not. A budget can anchor the quote and hide specification quality. Instead, define the specification clearly and compare complete responses. If the market price is far from your budget, adjust the specification.
Q: How long should an LED display RFQ be valid?
A: Request a quote validity of 30-45 days. Component and freight costs change, and a short validity forces factories to commit to real numbers instead of placeholder pricing.
Q: What should I do if factories change the RFQ specification in their response?
A: Reject the change or request a new quotation. A factory that substitutes a different pixel pitch or cabinet is telling you it wants the order without meeting the specification. Keep the baseline controlled.
